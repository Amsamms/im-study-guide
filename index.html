Mona was a mechanical engineer who had spent ten years running production lines before her father asked her to take over Wahat Foods, the family company in Alexandria that packed dates and made date bars. Sales in Egypt had stopped growing, and her father wanted to sell abroad. Mona started by asking what that really meant. International marketing, she learned, is the process of planning and doing deals across national borders where something is exchanged. A company can do it in several ways: by exporting its goods, by licensing another firm to use its name or recipe, by forming a strategic alliance with a foreign partner, by buying a foreign company outright (an acquisition), or by building its own operation abroad, which is called foreign direct investment. Whatever the route, the company has to live with four big outside forces in each country: its political system, its legal framework, its cultural norms and its economic forces.

The driving forces pushing firms like hers outward were market needs that were similar across countries, technology, economies of scale (the bigger you produce, the cheaper each unit gets) and easier communication. Her own reasons fitted the usual list: developing a new opportunity, using abroad the strengths that made Wahat win at home, following customers who had moved abroad, spreading risk through diversification, chasing economies that grow faster than Egypt's, selling a product that was old news at home but new elsewhere (product life cycle differences), and defending the company before foreign rivals came to Egypt. Two restraining forces held her back: national markets differ, so some adaptation is almost always needed, and most governments control who may enter their markets and how. She also saw five things that had made world trade grow. The first was managers changing their outlook, from ethnocentric (our home way is best), to polycentric (each country is different), to regiocentric (treat a whole region as one), to geocentric (think of the whole world as one market). The others were an international money system with floating exchange rates and the IMF; a world trading system shaped by GATT (the world agreement to cut import taxes) and trade blocs such as the EU, ASEAN, NAFTA and APEC; cheaper transport thanks to containers; and technology such as electronic data interchange, where companies swap orders and invoices computer to computer.

Her father asked how companies usually go international. Mona found four schools of thought. The stages approach says firms start with the entry mode that needs the least money and effort, then commit more as they gain experience. The learning approach agrees it is a gradual build-up but adds psychic distance, how foreign a market feels, so firms first go where things feel familiar. The contingency approach says a firm simply grabs an opportunity when it appears, near or far, easy or hard. The network approach, described by Johanson and Mattsson, says what matters is a firm's relationships, and it moves through international extension (first links abroad), international penetration (deeper links) and international integration (a full web of links across countries). Underneath all of it sat the core ideas of marketing itself: people have needs, which become wants, which become demands when backed by money; products meet them; exchange and transactions happen in markets. Mona drew this as a wheel. In the middle sat the four things she controlled, product, price, promotion and channels of distribution. Around them sat the home environment she could not control, the economic climate, the legal structure and the political forces, which stayed fixed. Around that sat a ring for each foreign market, which changed from country to country: economic forces, competitive forces, the level of technology, the structure of distribution, geography and infrastructure, cultural forces and political and legal forces.

Culture worried her most, so she studied it next. Culture is the human part of a person's surroundings: the beliefs, morals, customs and habits people learn from others. It is learned, not born; it is dynamic, so it changes; it is subjective, so it looks different from inside and outside; and it tells people in a community which behaviour is acceptable. For a marketer, the cultural points that matter are knowledge of the culture, sensitivity to it, how collective people are, their social conventions and their cognitive styles, meaning how they think and decide. Some things are shared by every culture. Physically, everyone wants to look beautiful and to keep track of time. Socially, everyone wants to cooperate, to belong to a group and to rank people by status. Emotionally, every culture has courtship, religious observance and mourning. Yet the elements of culture differ: material culture (tools and technology), social institutions such as family and schools, relations with the universe (religion and beliefs), aesthetics (taste in beauty, colour and music) and language. Culture shows itself through symbols (words, gestures, objects or pictures with special meaning), through norms and rituals people do together, and through myths and heroes, people living or dead who embody what that culture prizes.

Mona liked the idea that culture is a collective fingerprint: a mark of identity, not of superiority. Keillor's National Identity Scale measures that identity on four dimensions: national heritage, cultural homogeneity (how alike people are), belief system and consumer ethnocentrism, which is the feeling that buying foreign goods is wrong. Culture also works on three levels. National culture shapes dealings with governments and the values behind laws, which is why in some countries nepotism and cronyism (favouring relatives and friends) let wrongdoers escape punishment. Industry culture shapes the norms of a trade, such as credit policy and whether kickbacks (secret payments to win a deal) are expected. Organisational culture shapes dealings with firms, for example in alliances or takeovers. Hofstede found that national cultures differ mainly in values, while organisations differ mainly in practices.

Four ideas helped her explain cultural differences. The first was Maslow's hierarchy of needs, a ladder from basic to higher needs. In the West it runs from physiological needs, to safety, to belonging, to prestige, to self-actualisation (becoming your best self). In Asia the upper steps read differently: physiological, safety, affiliation, admiration and finally status. The second was the self-reference criterion: the unconscious habit of judging a foreign situation by your own culture's standards, the cultural baggage every businessperson carries. Culture does not decide the outcome of a deal, but it shapes the process. Lee gave four steps to remove this bias: define the problem in terms of your own culture, define it again in terms of the foreign culture, isolate the self-reference part and see how it complicates things, then redefine the problem without it and solve it. Mona smiled at the old joke on stereotyping, that heaven is where the cooks are French, the mechanics German, the police English, the lovers Italian and the Swiss organise everything, while hell swaps them all around; it showed how lazy labels mislead. The third idea was context. In a high-context culture, the words carry only part of the meaning, and the rest comes from how, where and by whom it is said, including body language. In a low-context culture, the words themselves carry most of the meaning. The fourth was psychic distance, how far apart two markets feel, as opposed to map distance, created by differences in language, education, politics, level of development and culture. Firms tend to trade first with countries that feel close.

She noted the everyday differences that trip people up: time, personal space, language, familiarity, consumption patterns and business customs. Verbal communication differs in who speaks to whom, what is said, how, where, when and why. Morris listed the non-verbal signals worth watching: body stress signals, lower body signals, posture, random gestures and facial expressions. Cateora named ten qualities that help a person adapt abroad: open tolerance, flexibility, humility, a sense of justice and fairness, adjusting to different tempos, curiosity, knowledge of the country, liking others, the ability to command respect and the ability to blend into the environment. Cultures can be compared two at a time (bilateral comparisons) or all at once (global comparisons). The best known global comparison is Hofstede's survey of IBM staff, which found four dimensions: power distance, how far less powerful people accept that power is shared unequally; uncertainty avoidance, how threatened people feel by the unknown; individualism versus collectivism, whether people look after themselves and their close family with loose ties to others, or belong to tight groups; and masculinity versus femininity, how different the social roles of men and women are. Later research added a fifth, long-term versus short-term orientation, meaning a practical, future-focused view versus a view rooted in the past and the present. Mona also wrote down the social side of doing business abroad: social sensitivity, good corporate citizenship and ethical behaviour.

Her first test came in a negotiation with a buyer from a high-context country. She had read the background of negotiation and learned the iceberg principle: what you see across the table is only the tip, and most of what drives the other side lies hidden below, so assuming their norms match yours is a mistake. Culture shapes negotiations through different ways of thinking, self-esteem and "face" (not being embarrassed in public), value systems, the right degree of formality, and the balance of harmony and emotion. To prepare, she set a strategy, chose her team carefully and researched the context. The talks then went through five stages. In the pre-negotiation stage the team and goals were set and the two sides built rapport, which takes longer with high-context partners. In the opening stage they got down to business, with the right formality and the exchange of name cards. In the bargaining stage she applied her strategy and decided whether to agree the broad deal first or each point separately. In the concession and agreement stage, clashing styles appeared, her own hurry against the buyer's patience. In the post-negotiation stage both sides felt cognitive dissonance, the nagging doubt about whether they had decided well. Throughout, the atmosphere depended on how much each side focused on the other, their feelings toward each other, openness versus secrecy, and willingness to adjust culturally. She also saw that negotiators differ in style. She learned that cultural sensitivity means actively accepting another culture, not just tolerating it, yet it does not mean giving up your own ways, only knowing local customs and adjusting where differences cause misunderstanding.

Next she faced the two hardest questions: which countries to enter, and how. The choice of market matters because it can affect profits at home as well as abroad. A small firm and a large one face it differently. She could enter markets one by one (incremental) or several at once (simultaneous), and she could concentrate on a few or diversify across many. Inexperienced firms usually find markets by chance, opportunistically, while experienced firms move toward systematic searching. The chance stimuli are typical: an unexpected inquiry or order, discovering potential during a trip abroad, information passed on by third parties, and trade missions or trade fairs. Mona's company had received exactly such an unsolicited order from Germany, but she wanted a system.

The aim of screening is to arrive at a portfolio of attractive markets. One method uses grids that compare how attractive each market is with how competitive the firm would be there, but analysing every country this way takes time that small firms rarely have. The alternative starts with all the world's markets and passes them through a series of criteria, dropping unsuitable ones at each step. For both new and experienced firms, strategic aims also shape the choice: building a competitive position, transferring risk and weighing the investment a market needs against the profit it offers. Mona used a systematic four-filter model. The first filter, macro-level research on general market potential, looked at economic statistics, social structure, the political and legal environment and geography. The second looked at the general market for her kind of product: growth trends for similar products, market size, the stage of development and whether data existed. Market size can be judged by geographic indicators (area, climate, terrain), demographic indicators (total population, its growth rate, age spread and density) and economic indicators (total and per person GNP, the value of a country's yearly output; per person income and its growth; disposable household income, money left after tax; and how evenly income is spread). Stage of development follows the life cycle of introduction, growth, maturity and decline. The third filter, micro-level research, looked at existing and potential competition, how reliable the information was, sales projections and profit potential. To estimate her own sales potential she checked local import laws, competition, the local market and channel structure, physical distance from Egypt, and language and cultural differences. The final filter judged target markets on cultural acceptance of the product, the ease and cost of entry, and taxes and duties. At each filter, rejected countries dropped away, the survivors were graded as preliminary, possible and probable opportunities, and in the end she had a country priority listing.

She saw that three things influence any firm's selection: company size, company strategy and how much market information is available. Strategy may even put profit second, such as getting rid of surplus production, or it may aim at stable sales with growth, a target percentage of profit on international sales, or growing market share in each country. Her information came from inside the firm, the knowledge and experience of her staff and the company's own data, and from outside: published references, trade journals, magazines and newspapers, government or industry advisers and support services, trade associations, business clubs, consultants, market research agencies and market intelligence services. Then came the entry mode. Export-based entry covers indirect exporting through a middleman, direct exporting, opening a sales office abroad, licensing and franchising (renting out a whole business format). Manufacturing-based entry covers a joint venture (shared ownership with a local partner), an acquisition and a greenfield operation, which means building from nothing. Relationship-based entry covers contract manufacturing (paying a local firm to make the goods), strategic alliances and countertrade, where goods are paid for with other goods. Every mode trades off control against the resources committed: more control costs more money and effort. Her choice also depended on whether she wanted a penetration (low price) or a skimming (high price) strategy, how complex the market was, her experience and resources, whether the market was similar or distant, how much commitment, flexibility, control and speed she wanted, political risk, competition and her goals. She also saw that internationalisation can be outward driven (selling abroad), inward driven (buying from abroad first) or linked (both together). She began by exporting, since exporting is one of the simplest ways in.

Now she had to decide what exactly to sell. A product, she learned, is a constellation of attributes, physical, service or symbolic, that satisfy the buyer, not just a thing with a shape, size, colour and form. Her product strategy had four parts: understanding how products spread and get adopted abroad, redefining the business, leveraging capabilities and understanding the structure of the product market. Products spread according to five things. Customer benefits differ because people's needs differ. Customer segments abroad may not be the ones she targeted at home. Technology may be too advanced or too simple for a market, depending on education, infrastructure, research environment, attitudes to innovation and the cost of labour and materials. The value chain, Porter's idea that production adds value by turning cheap inputs into more valuable outputs, may not exist abroad in the same efficient form. And the product life cycle differs; Onkvisit and Shaw describe an international version with five stages: local innovation, international innovation, maturity, worldwide imitation and reversal, when the original country ends up importing the product. To leverage capabilities she could share physical assets, use contract manufacturing, share external relationships, use established networks, share information and expertise, and spread development costs. Understanding product market structure meant three things. First, market boundaries differ by the product's use, by what the category includes and by competitors' activities. Second, differentiation and branding, such as changing pack size or the claimed use. Third, the market's dimensions: a mature market is mostly replacement buying with low potential while a growth market, especially a big and fast-growing one, offers much more.

She found many reasons to modify a product: product standards and regulations, measurement and calibration, trademarks, climate and usage, language and symbolism, style, design and taste, technology and performance standards, and warranty and servicing. For poorer countries, Robinson listed the factors in developing a product: the level of technical skills, labour costs, literacy, income, interest rates and maintenance, plus climate, isolation and different standards. The big debate was standardisation, selling the same product everywhere, versus adaptation. Standardisation is encouraged by a high cost of adapting, the nature of the product, tastes converging between countries, and economies in manufacturing, research, marketing and integration between countries. Adaptation is encouraged by competition, varied needs and tastes, different conditions of use, government influence and legal requirements. A third path is to design a global product from the start, for home and foreign markets at almost the same time. Mona also supplied a few factories, and learned that industrial products are different: they are bought to make other products, their demand is derived from consumer demand (a factory buys only because shoppers buy its end product), it is often reciprocal (I buy from you, you buy from me), and it often rests with a few buyers. Bonoma described the people in an industrial purchase as the initiator, the decider, the influencer, the gatekeeper who controls information, the user and the purchaser. Selling industrial goods abroad involves different buyers, different market potential, targeting the real decision makers, government control, service support, direct contact between buyer and seller, and terms of sale.

Branding came next. Kotler defines a brand as a name, term, sign, symbol or design, or a mix of them, that identifies one seller's goods and sets them apart from competitors. A brand image breathes life into a lifeless product, giving it a personality, and creates an invisible but magnetic bond with the consumer. A brand works through functionality (size, capacity, performance) and representation (image and symbolism). Firms build brands because a brand is legal property protected from attack, a way to identify a product so consumers can ask for it, reject it or recommend it, a shorthand that saves a long choice each time, a promise of consistent quality and a way for buyers to project their self-image. Branding confirms legal protection, guarantees quality when buyer and seller no longer meet face to face, and differentiates. Strong brands bring loyalty, a price premium, market share, economies of scale, a way over entry barriers, brand extensions, competitive advantage, bargaining power and control. But brand building faces high cost, risk, lack of marketing skill and small market size, and it needs functional capacity, symbolism, a name, a sign of ownership, a shorthand notation, legal protection and strategic direction. A good brand name suggests the product's benefits or qualities, is easy to say, recognise and remember, is distinctive, and can work internationally if needed, and Nova and Irish Mist were the examples that reminded her to check how a name sounds in every language before using it abroad.

Mona weighed four brand strategies. Individual brands give each product its own name, which isolates the company's reputation from a failure and matches each name to its product, but needs heavy promotion. Family brands put the company name on everything, often together with a product name, so new products cost less to launch and sell immediately, but one failure hurts the whole family. A multibrand option offers several brands in one category, gaining shelf space, catching customers who switch brands, creating healthy competition inside the firm and serving different segments. Umbrella marketing lets a new product borrow the strength of an established one under a varied marketing programme, as SAAB-Scania linked its cars to its sophisticated jet fighters in advertising. She also noted the traps in international branding: packaging and labelling rules differ, products need instructions in the local language, private-label makers (shops' own brands) imitate famous brands with similar packs, shapes and colours, and a good product sells poorly without repair or replacement service. Brands, she learned, have value beyond profit: they improve corporate image, stretch to new products and open difficult markets, and they are legal property with a market price. Brand equity is the extra value that a brand's name and symbol add to the product. It is whatever would vanish if the same product were sold without that name; in money terms, the income that loyal customers bring. It is built by making customers more familiar with the brand and better informed about it. Finally, her packaging had three jobs: protection, promotion and convenience.

With the product ready, she had to tell people about it. Promotion is communication: sending a message from the seller to the buyer. The sender, a person, a brand or a company, encodes the message (turns the idea into words and pictures) to suit the medium and audience; the medium can be anything from a letter or directory to a billboard or television advert, and must fit both the environment and the product; the receiver decodes (interprets) it and responds; feedback shows whether the objectives are met, sometimes immediately. Receivers include employees, intermediaries, media editors, decision makers and present and future customers. Interference creates noise, often from competitors sending messages at the same time, and environmental factors matter: how well local tastes, social norms, religious customs and standards of behaviour are respected. Communication works best when the source has a monopoly of attention, when the message fits what receivers already believe, when it targets familiar, lightly held, side issues, when the source seems expert, high in status, objective or likeable, and when the receiver's group supports it. Mona followed the steps: identify the audience, set objectives, design the message, choose the channel, set the total budget, decide the promotion mix, measure results, and manage and coordinate it all.

She hit the constraints quickly: language differences, what is acceptable in marketing, which media and intermediaries were available, competitors' activities and government controls. Six factors make international communication different from domestic: culture, language, education, economic development, media infrastructure and government regulation. In a high-context culture an advert should imply rather than state the obvious, and in a low-context culture the reverse; Dulek groups this into conversational, presentation and written-word principles. Translating words does not guarantee the same meaning. Where literacy is low, messages must be visual or spoken. Economic development decides who can afford the product, who needs it and how sophisticated it can be. Media differ in availability, reach, cost and effectiveness, and even the same medium may come in a different form. Governments regulate which products can be promoted, what is said, by whom, how, when and in which medium. Many blunders come from translating words literally instead of meaning, as Mike Kelly's collection of hotel notices shows, such as the Japanese hotel that invited guests to take advantage of the chambermaid and the Norwegian bar that requested ladies not to have children in the bar.

Mona managed her promotion in six steps: isolate the communication problem, identify the target population, define the objective, set the creative strategy, design the media plan, then implement and monitor the campaign. She kept in mind the buyer's decision process: need recognition, information search, evaluation and comparison, the purchase decision, and post-purchase evaluation. Her message had to decide between national and international appeals, allowing for linguistic and cultural nuances, different products, users and media choices, communication sophistication, business priorities and the status of marketing in each country. Pull strategies aim straight at end users, so that they ask shops for the product, mainly through advertising, which differs between countries in usage, form, content and presentation. Choosing media meant being specific about the target, the factors affecting media, the type of message and the campaign objective. Print media include newspapers, magazines, other print, and posters, billboards, signs and transit ads; visual media are television and cinema; audio media mean radio, which paints pictures with voice and other sounds; global media span print, visual, audio and the internet, and they ignore national borders. Language in mass media raised the use of foreign words, effects on advert layout and translation problems. Publicity and public relations added news releases, sponsorships, PR activity, loyalty programmes, agencies and checking advertising effectiveness.

Push strategies aim at the trade (wholesalers and retailers). Sales promotion matters far more abroad than at home. Trade shows are an affordable and convenient start for small firms, letting her meet buyers without appointments; they can be open to the public, trade-only, or trade days followed by public days, and general or specialised. To take part well she had to research, attract an audience, sort out infrastructure, understand why visitors come and measure results. Technical seminars suit products with technology new to a market. Personal selling abroad covers trade missions, the most common form, plus sales force activity, brochures and direct selling. A trade mission, a group of firms visiting a market together, felt like a series of blind dates; outward missions can be general, policy-related, product-category or project-related. Their drawbacks are competitors on the same mission, long planning, invitation-only places and fewer contacts than at trade displays; their benefits are market information, access to senior decision makers, contact with planners and regulators, publicity, relationship-building and synergy. Her sales staff learned to be prepared, slow the pace to local norms, not hurry decisions, watch body language and invest in relationships; her brochures needed cultural sensitivity, and direct mail worked at consumer and commercial levels. She also learned that "made in Egypt" could add to or subtract from her image, depending on how Egypt was seen for that product; with globalisation, "made in" means less than it once did. When buyers do not know a product, the country image acts as a halo that colours their judgement (the halo construct); when they know the country's products well, the image sums up their past experience (the summary construct). On standardising versus tailoring her adverts, she chose the doughnut approach: a standard outer ring with the brand name and a uniform global message, and a centre adapted to each country.

Pricing came next. Setting a price abroad needs research, and some costs, such as import duties, are beyond her control yet change how customers see value. Price is the only part of the marketing mix that brings in money; it affects demand, usually inversely (higher price, fewer buyers), and it shapes perceived value. Buyers judge prices against a reference price (the price they expect or compare with), and often treat price as a sign of quality. Prices can be changed quickly in a free market but not in a regulated one, and the price buys a whole bundle of other attributes. Pricing abroad is complex because it ties to overall strategy, competitors, demand and regulation. Mona listed what affected her prices: the nature of the product (technical or "me too", a copy of products already on sale; swinging raw material prices, service expectations, where it is made), the market's location and environment (research and visit costs, climate, economic factors), the distribution system (how much control and how long the channel), government rules (export inspection costs, meeting standards, local taxes), competition (substitutes, competitors' reactions and position) and culture (haggling, and loading the price to cover pay-offs, meaning bribes).

She chose among skimming (a high price for the few who value the product most), market pricing (matching the going price) and penetration pricing (a low price to win share fast), after asking which segments to target, who the competitors were and how strong, why and how customers buy, and which segments were largest. She learned the terms of sale that set where her responsibility ended: ex-works (the buyer collects at her factory), free alongside ship (she delivers to the quay beside the ship), free on board (she pays until the goods are on board), cost and freight (she also pays the sea freight), cost, insurance and freight (freight plus insurance), and delivered duty paid (she delivers to the buyer, duty paid). She learned the payment terms, from riskiest to safest for her: consignment (paid only after the goods sell), open account (goods first, invoice later), documents against acceptance (the buyer gets the shipping papers by signing a promise to pay later), documents against payment (the buyer pays to get the papers), letter of credit (the buyer's bank guarantees payment), confirmed letter of credit (a second bank also guarantees it) and cash in advance. To set the price she compared full-cost pricing, which covers all costs plus profit (cost-plus), with marginal-cost pricing, which covers only the extra cost of export sales and lets the marketplace guide the price. The best price lies between a ceiling, the most the market will pay, and a floor, the lowest price that covers her costs; with no positive gap the market should be dropped unless things are expected to improve. A wrong price sends the wrong quality signal to unfamiliar buyers. The Japanese approach does not take costs as given; it starts from the price that will win the market and forces costs down to fit. Another approach charges for the service rendered rather than for the physical product.

When the Egyptian pound moved, she learned about pass-through: how much of a currency change exporters pass on to buyers as higher or lower prices. It depends on the size of the export market's economy, how concentrated its industry is, whether the currency rose or fell, how many foreign exporters compete with local firms, the type and height of non-tariff barriers (obstacles other than import taxes, such as quotas) and the exporter's country of origin. When a rival cut prices, she could hold her price while she watched, cut it to keep customers, raise it and justify the rise, or cut it while adding perceived value. On the cost side she could use fewer intermediaries, drop costly features, ship components and assemble locally, change the product so it falls in a lower tariff class, value it for customs duty at marginal rather than full cost, so the duty is lower, manufacture in a cheaper country, or let grey markets develop (unofficial dealers reselling her goods cheaply). Five constraints limited her. The first was restrictive trade practices, such as horizontal price fixing between competitors, vertical price fixing along the channel, dividing markets, export or import cartels (firms agreeing prices together), boycotts, monopolies, mergers and price discrimination (charging different buyers different prices), which the law may ban, investigate after notification or allow. The second was dumping, selling abroad below the home price, whether predatory to kill rivals, sporadic to clear surplus, or unintentional. The others were devaluation and revaluation of currencies; administration pricing, where a government dictates prices to protect its citizens; and inflation, which forces regular price reviews. Globally she could charge standard world prices, market-differentiated prices or a modified mix of both. Transfer pricing is the price one part of a firm charges another part in a different country. Setting it low on goods sent from a high-tax country to a low-tax one means little profit is taxed in the first. It only arises when a firm invests and has divisions abroad, and it gives multinationals great influence.

The last piece was getting the dates to the shelves. A channel of distribution is an organised network of agencies linking producers with users, and distribution is the physical flow of goods through it; it can be a source of competitive advantage or of trouble. She could sell to export merchants in Egypt, who buy the goods, take ownership and ship them, so the sale feels domestic. She could export directly through an agent or distributor, the commonest route for small and medium firms. She could export intellectual content through franchising or licensing. Or she could manufacture abroad through assembly, often in a foreign trade zone, a special area with low duties and other government incentives; contract manufacture, where a local firm makes the goods for a fee with no rights to them; a joint venture, where she owns enough to have a say in management but not enough to dominate; acquisition of an existing plant or building one from scratch, which needs big investment, contacts and research; or a strategic alliance. Her choice weighed cost, control, commitment, the nature of the market and of the product, and her objectives. She described each channel by its length (how many levels of middlemen), width (how many intermediaries at each level), density (how much of the market it covers), alignment (who leads it) and logistics (how the goods physically move). Channel structures differ by country and decide strategy, pricing, and whether a pull or a push strategy fits. Channels bridge two gaps: physical distance, and the gap in attitudes and perceptions between buyer and seller. Government rules may force her to design around regulated parts of the system, and commercial limits included the intermediary role being held by a particular group, the breadth of lines intermediaries carry and the wider environment.

Managing the channel became her daily work. Since more than half of world trade passes through agents and distributors, selecting the right one was critical. The appointment needed a clear agreement naming the products and the territory. Communication and control aimed at a positive climate for a long relationship, and motivation, and if needed termination, rested on win-win rather than each side grabbing advantage. Her wholesalers handled purchasing, selling, transport, storage, financing and gathering information, while retailing varied by country in shop size, people served and its role in social life. She learned that distribution gets the least attention in the standardisation debate, which has two sides: process standardisation, uniform management practices, and programme standardisation, a uniform marketing mix. She also caught a grey market, unauthorised dealers buying her bars in countries where they were cheap and reselling them more cheaply than her official partners where prices were high. Physical distribution covered customer service, packaging, transport, warehousing and documents. Service abroad tends to be poorer than at home because of distance, more chances of mistakes, weak packaging, transport delays and customs problems; long journeys raise the risk of theft and damage, so packaging must protect. Transport has three legs: factory to port, port to foreign port, and arrival point to customer. Warehousing varies in size, practice and location. Every shipment needed four key documents: the export declaration to customs, the bill of lading (the shipping line's receipt and proof of ownership), the commercial invoice and the certificate of origin, proving where the goods were made.

On a cold morning in Hamburg, the first pallets of Wahat date bars, with German labels, a confirmed letter of credit and a distributor who saw the deal as good for both sides, reached the shelves. Mona knew that without delivery, the product, the price and the adverts would have counted for nothing.

Why a company would sell outside its home country, what pushes it to do so and what holds it back. It also gives the basic vocabulary used in the rest of the course.

Definition slide 4

International marketing is "the process of planning and undertaking transactions across national boundaries that involve exchange". In plain words: planning and doing deals that cross a border, where something is exchanged (goods or services for money).

Types of international marketing slide 5

Five types: Exporting, Licensing (letting a foreign firm use your brand or know-how for a fee), Strategic alliances (partnering with a foreign firm), Acquisitions (buying a foreign firm) and Foreign Direct Investment, FDI (building or owning operations abroad).

Macro environmental factors slide 6

The big outside forces in every country: Political systems, Legal frameworks, Cultural norms, Economic forces.

Driving forces slide 7

Four forces that push firms abroad: Market needs, Technology, Economies of scale (bigger volume means lower cost per unit), Communication.

Reasons for internationalisation slide 8

  • Opportunity development
  • Leveraging key success factors abroad (using what you are good at in new markets)
  • Follow customers abroad
  • Pursuing diversification (spreading risk over many markets)
  • Taking advantage of different growth rates of economies
  • Exploiting product life cycle differences (a product old at home may be new elsewhere)
  • Internationalising for defensive reasons (to protect yourself from competitors)

Restraining forces slide 9

Two forces hold firms back: Differences between national markets (some adaptation is usually needed) and Market control (most countries control entry and access to their markets).

Key factors in growth slide 10

  • Change in management orientation: ethnocentric (home country way is best), polycentric (each country treated separately), regiocentric (by region), geocentric (the whole world as one market).
  • International monetary framework: fluctuating (floating) exchange rates and the IMF (International Monetary Fund).
  • World trading system: GATT (the world agreement to cut import taxes), EU, ASEAN, NAFTA, APEC (trade agreements and trading blocs).
  • Communication and transport: e.g. containerisation (shipping in standard containers).
  • Technology: e.g. electronic data interchange (computers exchanging business documents).

Approaches to internationalisation slides 11-15

  • Stages approaches: the earliest theories. Start with the entry mode needing the least resources, then commit more as experience grows.
  • Learning approaches: internationalisation is dynamic, a gradual build up over time; recognises the role of psychic distance (how different a market feels from home).
  • Contingency approaches: the firm responds to each opportunity as it appears, whether the market feels close or far, and whether the entry mode needed is simple or advanced.
  • Network approaches: stress linkages and relationships. Johanson and Mattsson describe three positions: international extension (first links abroad), international penetration (deeper links in markets already entered), international integration (a full web of links across many countries).

Core marketing concepts slide 16

Needs, Wants, Demands, Products, Exchange, Transactions, Markets. In plain words: needs become wants, wants backed by money become demands, products meet them, and exchange and transactions happen in markets.

The wheel of international marketing slide 17

In the centre are the four things the firm controls: Product, Price, Promotion, Channels of distribution. Around them is the home environment the firm cannot control (political forces, legal structure, economic climate). Around that are the uncontrollable environments of each foreign market, which vary from market to market: political/legal, economic, competitive, level of technology, structure of distribution, geography and infrastructure, cultural forces.

True/False traps

  • There are five types of international marketing on slide 5; "franchising" is not one of them on this slide (it appears later under entry modes).
  • Restraining forces are only two: differences between markets and market control.
  • Network positions are extension, penetration, integration (not "expansion").
  • Stages approaches start with the LEAST commitment of resources, not the most.
  • In the wheel, the 4Ps are controllable; the environment is uncontrollable.

Chapter 3: Catering for the Cultural Environment of International Business

Culture shapes how people buy, talk and negotiate, so a marketer must understand it before selling abroad. The chapter gives tools to compare cultures and a guide to negotiating across them.

What culture is slides 3-4

Culture tells people which behaviour is acceptable in their community. It is learned, dynamic (it changes) and subjective. One definition: "the human aspect of a person's environment; it consists of beliefs, morals, customs, and habits learned from others". Key lesson: cultural behaviour is learnt, not inborn.

Impact of culture on marketing slide 5

Factors to consider: Knowledge, Sensitivity, Collectivism (putting the group before the individual), Social conventions, Cognitive styles (how people think and process information).

Cultural universals slides 6-7

Traits found in every culture, in three settings: Physical (wanting to look beautiful, keeping track of time), Social (cooperating, belonging to a group, ranking by status), Emotional (courtship, religious observance, mourning).

Elements of culture slide 8

Five: Material culture (tools, technology, things people own), Social institutions (family, schools, government), Relations with the universe (religion and beliefs), Aesthetics (sense of beauty, art, colour, music), Language.

Expressions of culture slide 9

  • Symbols: words, gestures, objects or pictures with a special meaning.
  • Norms and rituals: activities people do together.
  • Myths and heroes: people, alive or dead, with qualities the culture admires.

Culture as a collective fingerprint slide 10

Culture is a mark of identity, not of superiority. Keillor's National Identity Scale has four dimensions: National heritage, Cultural homogeneity (how alike people are), Belief system, Consumer ethnocentrism (preferring to buy home-country products).

Levels at which culture operates slides 11-14

  • National: affects dealings with governments, laws and institutions (explains why nepotism and cronyism, favouring relatives and friends, can go unpunished in some cultures).
  • Industry: norms of an industry in that country, e.g. credit policy, kickbacks (secret payments to win a deal).
  • Organisational: affects dealings with firms, e.g. alliances or takeovers.

Hofstede: at national level, differences lie more in values; at organisational level, more in practices.

Key cultural concepts slides 15-22

  • Maslow's hierarchy of needs: West (bottom to top): physiological, safety, belonging, prestige, self-actualisation. Asia: physiological, safety, affiliation, admiration, status. Used to see how consumers in different countries respond to cultural stimuli.
  • Self-reference criterion (SRC): the "cultural baggage" we carry; judging a foreign situation by our own culture. Culture does not decide the outcome, it influences the process. Lee's four steps to reduce it: (1) define the problem in your own cultural terms, (2) define it in the foreign market's terms, (3) isolate the SRC and see how it complicates things, (4) redefine the problem without the SRC and solve it.
  • Cultural stereotyping: the "Heaven and Hell" joke (French cooks, German mechanics... versus German police, French mechanics...) shows how stereotypes work.
  • Context and culture: in a high-context culture, words carry only part of the meaning; how, where and with what body language something is said matters. In a low-context culture, messages are explicit and the words carry most of the meaning.
  • Psychic distance: how different a market feels from home, based on linguistic, educational, political, developmental and cultural factors. Firms tend to do business with cultures that feel close.

Key cultural differences slide 23

Time, Space, Language, Familiarity, Consumption patterns, Business customs.

Cultural communication slides 24-27

  • Verbal communication: differs in who communicates to whom, what, how, where, when and why.
  • Non-verbal communication: Morris's most important signals: body stress signals, lower body signals, body posture signals, random gestures, facial gestures.
  • Cultural adaptation: Cateora's ten criteria: open tolerance, flexibility, humility, justice/fairness, adjustability to varying tempos, curiosity, knowledge of the country, liking of others, ability to command respect, ability to integrate into the environment.

Hofstede's dimensions slides 28-34

Cross-cultural comparisons can be bilateral (two countries) or global. Hofstede surveyed IBM worldwide and found four dimensions; a fifth was added later.

DimensionMeaning

Social aspects slide 35

Social sensitivity, Good corporate citizenship, Ethical considerations.

Culture and international negotiation slides 36-46

  • Iceberg principle: most of what drives a negotiation is hidden below the surface; assuming other countries share your norms is wrong.
  • Cultural factors in negotiating: different approaches to thinking, self-esteem and "face" (dignity, not being embarrassed), value systems, degree of formality, harmony and emotion.
  • Preparing: strategy, composition of the team, researching the context.
  • Five stages: (1) Pre-negotiation: form the team and objectives, build rapport; longer with high-context cultures. (2) Opening: get down to business, formality, name cards. (3) Bargaining: apply the strategy; decide broad agreement first or point by point. (4) Concession and agreement: clashing styles (impatience versus patience) cause problems. (5) Post-negotiation: cognitive dissonance (doubt about whether the deal was right).
  • Atmosphere: degree of mutual orientation (how much each side focuses on the other), feelings towards the other party, openness versus secrecy, willingness to make cultural adjustments.

Conclusion: cultural sensitivity means active acceptance, not just tolerance. You need not abandon your own ways, but you should know local customs and accommodate them.

True/False traps

  • Hofstede started with FOUR dimensions; long-term orientation was the fifth, added later.
  • High-context = meaning is in the setting and body language; low-context = meaning is in the words. Easy to swap.
  • Power distance is about accepting inequality; uncertainty avoidance is about fear of the unknown.
  • National level differences are in VALUES; organisational level differences are in PRACTICES.
  • Negotiation has FIVE stages; cognitive dissonance belongs to the post-negotiation stage, not bargaining.

Chapter 7: International Market Selection and Entry

Two key questions: which foreign market should the firm enter, and how should it enter it. The chapter shows how to screen countries and compares the ways of entering.

Market selection: experienced vs inexperienced firms Written question slides 2, 4-8, 11-12

Choosing a market affects profit at home as well as abroad. Approaches differ for small and large firms:

  • Incremental (one market at a time) or simultaneous (several at once).
  • Concentrated (focus on few markets) or diversified (spread over many).
  • Opportunistic identification: typical of beginners; reacting to chance. Stimuli: an unsolicited inquiry or order, a casual discovery during a trip abroad, information from third parties, trade missions and trade fairs. Firms evolve from opportunistic to systematic identification as they gain experience.
  • For both novice and experienced firms, selection is shaped by strategic objectives: establishing a competitive position, transferring (spreading) risk, rating market investment against profitability (is the profit worth the money needed?).

Full model answer in the Exam corner, Q1.

Screening and country attractiveness Written question slides 9-20

  • Grid approach: compares market attractiveness with the firm's competitiveness, country by country. Time consuming, often too much for small firms.
  • Screening approach: start with all markets and eliminate unsuitable ones step by step.

Systematic model, four filters (slide 13): Filter 1 macro research (general market potential), Filter 2 the general market relating to the product, Filter 3 micro research (specific factors affecting the product), Filter 4 the final choice of target markets and a country priority listing. Countries that fail a filter are rejected; the survivors are labelled preliminary, possible and probable opportunities on the way to becoming target markets. Rejected countries drop out at each filter.

  • Macro level: economic statistics, social structure, political/legal environment, geographic factors.
  • General market for the product: growth trends for similar products, market size, stage of development, availability of market data.
  • Market size indicators: geographic (area, climate, topography); demographic (total population, growth rate, age distribution, density); economic (total GNP, the value of a country's yearly output; per capita (per person) GNP; per capita income and its growth; disposable income, money left after tax; income distribution, how evenly income is spread).
  • Stage of development: life cycle stage: introduction, growth, maturity, decline.
  • Micro level: existing and potential competition, reliability of information, sales projections, profit potential.
  • Company sales potential criteria: local import legislation, competition, local market and channel structure, physical distance from home, language and cultural differences.
  • Implementation factors: cultural acceptance of products, ease and cost of entry, taxes and duties.
  • Influences on selection: company size, company strategy, availability of market information.
  • Company strategy influences: non-maximising aims, i.e. goals other than the highest profit (e.g. disposing of surplus production), sales stability with growth, percentage profit on international sales, growth in market share.

  • Internal: knowledge and experience within the firm, company data.
  • External: published references, trade journals, magazines, newspapers, government or industry advisers and support services, trade associations, business clubs, consultants, market research agencies, market intelligence.

Chapter 8: Modifying Products for International Markets

How products spread to other countries, when to change them for local buyers, and how industrial products differ from consumer goods. It ends with branding and packaging abroad.

The product slide 3

A product is a "constellation of attributes", physical, service or symbolic, that satisfy the buyer. Narrowly, it is something tangible with shape, size, colour and form.

International product strategies slide 4

Product diffusion and adoption (how a product spreads to new countries and gets taken up), Redefining the business (rethinking what business you are in), Leveraging capabilities (using what the firm already has), Understanding product market structure.

How products spread and what drives adoption Written question slides 5-11

  • Customer benefits: people have different needs and see products as meeting them to different degrees.
  • Customer segments: abroad, the firm may target different groups than at home.
  • Technology: may be too advanced or too simple for the market; depends on education, infrastructure, R&D, attitude to innovation, cost of production factors.
  • Value chain (Porter): production adds value by turning cheaper inputs into more valuable outputs; how well the local value chain works limits what the firm can do there.
  • Product life cycle: Onkvisit and Shaw's (spelled "Onkavist" on the slide) five international stages: local innovation, international innovation, maturity, worldwide imitation, reversal (the original country ends up importing).

Leveraging capabilities slide 12

Shared physical assets, contract manufacturing, shared external relationships, established networks, shared information and expertise, development costs.

Product market structure slides 13-16

  • Market boundaries differ by country in the product's use, what the category includes and competitors' activities.
  • Differentiation and branding: set the product apart, e.g. a different pack size or claimed use.
  • Dimensions: a mature market has low potential and is mainly replacement; a growth market (large population, fast growth) has much more potential.

Modifying products slides 17-18

Product standards and regulations, measurement and calibration, trademarks, climate and usage, language and symbolism, style/design/taste, technology and performance standards, warranty and servicing.

Developing a product for an international market Written question slide 19

Robinson's factors (for less developed countries): level of technical skills, labour costs, literacy, income, interest rates, maintenance, climate, isolation, different standards.

Standardisation versus adaptation slides 20-23

  • Encourage standardisation (same product everywhere): high cost of adaptation, nature of the product, convergence of tastes, economies of scale in manufacture, in R&D, in marketing, and in integration between countries.
  • Encourage adaptation (slide 22): competition, variation in needs and tastes, different usage conditions, government influence, legal requirements.
  • Global product: designed from the start to be sold at home and abroad at almost the same time.

Industrial products slides 24-26

  • Unique features: bought to make other products; demand is derived (comes from consumer demand); demand is often reciprocal (firms buy from each other); few buyers.
  • Bonoma's buying roles: initiator, decider, influencer, gatekeeper (controls information reaching the buyers), user, purchaser.
  • Market features: different buyer characteristics, market potential, targeting decision makers, government control, service support, direct buyer-seller contact, terms of sale.

Branding slides 27-37

Kotler: a brand is "a name, term, sign, symbol or design or combination of them" that identifies a seller's goods and differentiates them. A brand gives a product a personality and an invisible, magnetic relationship with the consumer. It has functionality (size, capacity, performance) and representation (image, symbolism).

  • Reasons for brand building: legal property protected from competitors, a differentiating device, a shorthand that saves a long choice process, a promise of consistent quality, a way to project self-image.
  • Benefits of branding: confirms legal protection, guarantees quality when buyer and seller no longer meet face to face, differentiates in competition.
  • Benefits of strong brands: customer loyalty, price premium (buyers pay more), market share, economies of scale, overcoming entry barriers, brand extensions, competitive advantage, bargaining power, control.
  • Impediments: high cost, risk, lack of marketing expertise, market size.
  • Prerequisites: functional capacity, symbolism, brand name, sign of ownership, shorthand notation, legal protection, strategic direction.
  • Good brand name: suggests benefits (Slimline), suggests qualities (Crisp and Dry), easy to say and remember (Omo), distinctive (Kodak), usable internationally; the slide's examples for this point are Nova and Irish Mist (check how a name reads in each language before using it abroad).

Traps, brand value and packaging slides 42-46

  • Traps in international branding: packaging and labelling rules, language of instructions, imitation by private labels (shops' own brands copying famous packs), lack of after sales service.
  • Market value of a brand: improves corporate image, can extend to other products, opens difficult markets; it is legally protected property.
  • Brand equity: the extra value a brand name adds beyond the product itself; built by improving customers' knowledge of the brand.

  • The international product life cycle has FIVE stages and ends with "reversal".

Chapter 10: Promotion in International Marketing

Promotion is communication from seller to buyer, and crossing borders makes it harder because of language, culture, media and law. The chapter covers the communication process, advertising, pull and push tools, and country of origin.

Communication process slides 4-12

Promotion transmits a message from seller to buyer. Elements: sender, encoding (turning the idea into a message), message, media, decoding (the receiver interpreting it), receiver, response, feedback, noise.

  • Sender: a person, brand or company.
  • Medium: anything from a letter to a TV ad; must suit the environment and the product.
  • Receivers: employees, intermediaries, media editors, decision makers, present and future customers.
  • Interference: creates noise; a major source is competitors' messages at the same time.
  • Environmental factors: local tastes, social norms, religious mores (customs), standards of behaviour.
  • Feedback: shows whether objectives are being met; can be immediate.

What makes communication effective slide 13

A monopoly of the message (no competing messages); fitting the receiver's existing beliefs; best on familiar, lightly felt, side issues; a source seen as expert, high status, objective or likeable; the social or reference group (the people whose opinion the receiver follows) accepting it.

Steps in developing communication slide 14

Identify the target audience, set objectives, design the message, select the channel, set the budget, decide the promotion mix, measure results, manage and coordinate.

Constraints and factors in international communication Written question slides 15-23

  • Constraints (slide 15): language differences, marketing acceptability, media availability, intermediary availability, activities of competitors, government controls.
  • Factors (slide 16): culture (high-context: imply; low-context: state directly; Dulek's conversational, presentation and written word principles), language (translation loses meaning), education (low literacy needs visual or oral messages), economic development, media infrastructure, government regulation (of the product, content, who says it, how, when, and which medium).

Full model answer in the Exam corner, Q8.

Content and translation slides 24-25

Many blunders come from translating words literally instead of meaning. Mike Kelly's hotel sign examples, e.g. "You are invited to take advantage of the chambermaid" (Japan).

Managing promotion slides 26-28

  • Six steps: isolate the communication problem, identify the target population, define the objective, establish the creative strategy, design the media plan, implement and monitor.
  • Buyer decision process: need recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase decision, post-purchase evaluation.
  • Message strategy varies with linguistic and cultural nuances, products, users, media alternatives, communication sophistication, business priorities, status of marketing.

Pull strategies slides 29-38

Pull = aimed directly at end customers so they ask for the product.

  • Advertising: between-country differences in usage, form, content, presentation; media selection by target, factors, message type, campaign objective.
  • Print media: newspapers, magazines, other print, posters/billboards/signage/transit. Visual: television, cinema. Audio: radio. Global media: global print, visual, audio, internet.
  • Language in mass media: use of foreign words, impact on layout, translation problems.
  • Publicity and PR: news releases, sponsorships, PR activity, loyalty programmes, agencies, evaluating advertising effectiveness.

Push strategies slides 39-47

Push = pushing the product through the trade (wholesalers and retailers). Sales promotion matters MORE internationally than at home; in personal selling, trade missions are the most common activity.

  • Trade shows: affordable for small first-time exporters; meet buyers without appointments; public or trade-only; general or specialised. Effective participation: research, attracting an audience, infrastructure, visitor motivations, measuring outcomes.
  • Technical seminars: useful for technology new to the market.
  • Trade missions (a group of firms visiting a market together, "a series of blind dates"): general, policy-related, product-category, project-related. Advantages: market information, access to senior decision makers, contact with planners and regulators, publicity, relationship building, synergy. Disadvantages: competitors may be on the mission, long planning, often invitation only, fewer contacts than trade displays.
  • Salesforce tips: be prepared, slow down, do not hurry decisions, watch body language, build relationships. Brochures need cultural sensitivity; direct mail at consumer and commercial levels.

Country of origin and standardisation slides 48-49

  • Country of origin can add to or hurt a product's image. Halo construct: buyers who do not know the product judge it by the country's image. Summary construct: buyers who know the country's products sum up their experience into a country image.
  • Standardisation vs differentiation: globalisation argues for standard ads (economies of scale, same needs). Doughnut approach: a standard ring (brand name, global message) with a local core for each country.

True/False traps

  • Sales promotion plays a MORE important role internationally than domestically.
  • Trade missions are the most common personal selling activity abroad, but they give FEWER contacts than trade displays.
  • Noise is created by interference, mainly competitors' messages.
  • High-context: imply rather than state; low-context: state directly.
  • Halo = judging an unknown product by the country image; summary = country image built from known products.

Chapter 11: International Pricing for Profit

Price is the only part of the marketing mix that brings in money, and abroad it is pushed around by duties, currencies, governments and competitors. The chapter covers strategies, terms of sale and payment, price setting methods and constraints.

Importance and role of pricing Written question slides 3-9

  • Role: the only marketing mix variable that produces revenue; affects demand, usually inversely (higher price, lower demand); linked to perceived value.
  • Reference prices: buyers judge other products against a reference price (the price they expect or compare with); price is an indicator of quality.
  • Nature of the decision: can be changed at short notice, depending on whether the market is free or regulated.
  • Price element: includes a bundle of other attributes (the buyer pays for more than the physical product).
  • Complexities: overall strategy, competitors, level of demand, government regulations.

Full model answer in the Exam corner, Q9.

Factors in international pricing slides 10-16

  • Nature of product/industry: technical or "me too" (a copy of existing products), fluctuating raw material prices, service expectations, location of production.
  • Location and environment: research costs and visits, climate, economic factors.
  • Distribution system: degree of control, length of channel.
  • Government regulations: export inspection costs, conforming to standards, local taxes.
  • Competition: substitute prices, competitors' reaction, competitor position.
  • Culture: price negotiations, loading the price to cover pay-offs (bribes).

Pricing strategy slides 17-21

  • Strategies: skimming (start high), market pricing (match the market), penetration (start low to win share).
  • Planning questions: which segments, who are the main competitors and their strengths, why and how consumers buy, what the main segments are.
  • Terms of sale (who pays transport and risk up to where): Ex-works (buyer collects at the factory), Free alongside ship (seller delivers next to the ship), Free on board (seller loads onto the ship), Cost and freight (seller pays freight to destination port), Cost, insurance and freight (plus insurance), Delivered duty paid (seller delivers to the buyer, duties paid).
  • Terms of payment (riskiest to safest for the seller): consignment (paid only after the goods are sold), open account (goods first, invoice later), documents against acceptance (buyer gets documents by promising to pay later), documents against payment (buyer pays to get documents), letter of credit (buyer's bank guarantees payment), confirmed letter of credit (a second bank also guarantees), cash in advance.

Setting the price slides 22-30

  • Full vs marginal cost: full-cost or cost-plus pricing (all costs plus a margin) versus marginal-cost pricing (cover only the extra cost of the export units and let the marketplace set the price).
  • Optimal price: if there is no gap between the ceiling (highest price the market accepts) and the floor (lowest price covering cost), abandon the market unless things should improve.
  • Mismanagement: price signals quality, so a wrong price sends the wrong signal.
  • Japanese approach: costs are not a given; start from the price that will win the market, then decide the cost needed to reach it.
  • Alternative approach: charge for the service rendered, not just the product.
  • Currency fluctuations: "pass-through" (how much of an exchange rate change the exporter passes on to the importer as a higher or lower price) depends on size of the economy, industry concentration, appreciation or depreciation, share of foreign exporters, non-tariff barriers (obstacles other than import taxes, e.g. quotas), exporter's country of origin.
  • Responding to price changes: hold the price, cut it, raise and justify it, or cut it and add value. Cost-side responses: fewer intermediaries, drop costly features, ship components and assemble locally, modify to get a lower tariff, value the goods for customs duty at marginal cost (so duty is lower), manufacture where cheaper, allow grey markets (unofficial dealers reselling the goods cheaply).

Constraints on setting prices slides 31-37

  • Restrictive trade practices: horizontal price fixing (between competitors), vertical price fixing (between supplier and reseller), dividing markets, export/import cartels (firms agreeing prices together), boycotts, monopolies, mergers, price discrimination (charging different buyers different prices). Law may prohibit them, require notification and investigation, or allow them.
  • Dumping (selling abroad below home price or cost): predatory (to kill competitors), sporadic (occasional, to clear surplus), unintentional (caused by currency or timing).
  • Devaluation and revaluation: a currency officially losing or gaining value; the exporter cannot control it.
  • Administration pricing: the foreign government dictates the price, because its duty is to its citizens.
  • Inflation: prices must be adjusted periodically.

Global vs local pricing Written question slides 38-40

  • Standard world prices: one price everywhere.
  • Market differentiated prices: a different price for each market.
  • Modified pricing policy: a middle way between the two.
  • Transfer pricing: pricing between a firm's own subsidiaries, e.g. low prices to a low-tax country so little profit appears in the high-tax one. Happens with international investment, not simple exporting.

Full model answer in the Exam corner, Q10.

True/False traps

  • Price is the ONLY marketing mix element that produces revenue; price and demand are usually INVERSELY related.
  • Skimming = high starting price; penetration = low starting price.
  • Dumping has three forms: predatory, sporadic, unintentional.
  • Transfer pricing does NOT happen with simple exporting.
  • No positive gap between ceiling and floor means abandon the market (unless conditions should improve).

Chapter 12: Effective International Distribution

If the product cannot reach the customer, nothing else matters. The chapter compares ways to get goods into foreign markets, how to manage the partners who carry them, and the physical side of moving goods.

Definition slide 4

A channel of distribution is an organised network of agencies linking producers with users; distribution is the physical flow of goods through them. Channels can be a source of competitive advantage or of problems.

Alternative channels Written question slides 5-12

  • Export intermediary: export merchants at home buy the goods, take title (ownership) and ship them; for the firm it is like a domestic sale.
  • Direct export: the firm exports itself, usually through an agent or distributor; the commonest entry for small and medium firms.
  • Exporting intellectual content: franchising, licensing.
  • Manufacturing abroad: assembly (often in a foreign trade zone, a special area with low duties and government incentives); contract manufacture (a local firm makes the goods for a fee, with no rights to the output); joint venture (enough equity, i.e. ownership, to have a say, not to dominate); acquisition (buying or building a plant, big investment); strategic alliances.

Full model answers in the Exam corner, Q11 and Q13.

Chapter 7: International Market Selection and Entry

Q1. What are the different problems faced in selecting international markets by firms inexperienced and experienced in international business?

Chapter 7, slides 2, 4-8, 10-12, 21-23

Choosing which foreign country to sell in is one of the two most critical questions a firm faces when going abroad (the other is how to enter). A wrong choice can hurt profits not only abroad but also at home, and the problems differ between a beginner firm and an experienced one.

PointInexperienced firm (beginner, often small or medium)Experienced firm (often large)
How the market is foundOpportunistic: it reacts to chance, such as an unexpected order, a market noticed on a trip, tips from third parties, or a trade fairSystematic: it searches and compares markets on purpose, step by step

For both types of firm, the choice is shaped by:

  1. Strategic objectives: building a competitive position, spreading (transferring) risk, and weighing the investment in a market against the profit it brings.
  2. Company size: bigger firms have more people and money to study and enter markets.
  3. Company strategy: for example selling off surplus production, wanting steady sales with growth, a target profit percentage abroad, or growing market share.
  4. Availability of market information: where data is poor, selection is harder for everyone.

In short, beginners select by chance and with little data, while experienced firms select by system, but both must link the choice to their goals and resources.

Memory hook: beginner = "chance, one by one, few, screen"; expert = "system, many at once, spread, grid".

Q2. How can a country's attractiveness as an international market be assessed in terms of market potential, membership of trading blocs, competitive intensity, and entry barriers?

Chapter 7, slides 2, 9-20 (trading blocs: Chapter 1, slide 10)

Country attractiveness means how worthwhile a foreign market is for the firm. It is judged by passing all countries through a series of filters, moving from general facts to facts about the specific product, until a short priority list of target markets remains.

  1. Market potential (macro level, filter 1): general facts about the country: economic statistics, social structure, political and legal environment, and geography. Weak countries are rejected early.
  2. Market size indicators: geography (area, climate, terrain), people (total population, growth rate, age mix, density) and money (total and per-person national product (GNP), income and its growth, disposable income (money left after tax), how evenly income is spread).
  3. Market for this product (filter 2): growth trends of similar products, market size, the product's life-cycle stage there (introduction, growth, maturity, decline), and whether market data exists.
  4. Trading bloc membership: whether the country belongs to a trade group such as GATT (the world agreement to cut import taxes), the EU, ASEAN, NAFTA or APEC, which can open (or close) access to a bigger group of countries.
  5. Competitive intensity (micro level, filter 3): existing and potential competitors, how reliable the information is, expected sales and profit potential.
  6. Company sales potential: local import laws, competition, the local distribution set-up, physical distance from home, and language and cultural differences.
  7. Entry barriers (filter 4, implementation): whether the culture accepts the product, how easy and costly entry is, and taxes and duties.

The countries that survive all filters form a portfolio (a chosen set) of the most attractive markets, ranked in a country priority list.

Memory hook: "General country, then product market, then competition, then barriers" = 4 filters from wide to narrow.

Q3. What are the information sources used in international market selection?

Chapter 7, slide 23

To choose a foreign market a firm needs facts, which come from two places: inside the firm and outside it.

Internal sources (inside the firm):

  1. Knowledge and experience within the firm: what managers and staff already know from past dealings, travel or previous exports.
  2. Company data: the firm's own records, such as sales figures, customer enquiries and costs.

External sources (outside the firm):

  1. Published references: reference books and directories about countries and markets.
  2. Trade journals, magazines and newspapers: news and reports about industries and countries.
  3. Government or industry advisers and support services: export promotion bodies and official advisers who help firms going abroad.
  4. Trade associations, business clubs, consultants, market research agencies and market intelligence: organisations that collect or sell information and contacts.

Beginner firms lean more on external, often free, sources; experienced firms also have rich internal knowledge.

Memory hook: "Inside = what we know + our data; Outside = read it, ask government, ask associations and agencies".

Chapter 8: Modifying Products for International Markets

Q4. How do products spread to international markets?

Chapter 8, slides 4-5, 11-16

A product spreads abroad through diffusion (moving from country to country) and adoption (people in each new country starting to buy it). The international product strategies behind this are:

  1. Product diffusion and adoption: the product moves into new countries as customers there see a benefit in it (explained further in Q5).
  2. Redefining the business: the firm rethinks what business it is in, so the product can fit the needs of new markets.
  3. Leveraging capabilities: using what the firm already has to enter new markets more cheaply: shared physical assets, contract manufacturing (paying another firm to make the goods), shared outside relationships, established networks, shared information and expertise, and spreading development costs.
  4. Understanding product market structure: knowing where the market's boundaries lie in each country (the product's use, what the category includes, what competitors do), how to differentiate and brand the product, and the market's life-cycle stage.

The spread usually follows the international product life cycle (Onkvisit and Shaw, spelled "Onkavist" on the slide), five stages:

  1. Local innovation: the product is invented and sold at home.
  2. International innovation: it starts being exported.
  3. Maturity: demand abroad grows and steadies.
  4. Worldwide imitation: producers in other countries copy it.
  5. Reversal: the original home country ends up importing the product from the copiers.

Memory hook: strategies "Diffuse, Redefine, Leverage, Understand"; life cycle "Local, International, Mature, Imitate, Reverse".

Q5. What factors influence the adoption of products by consumers in international markets?

Chapter 8, slides 5-11

Adoption means customers in a foreign country accepting and starting to buy a product. Five factors decide how fast and how far this happens:

  1. Customer benefits: people in different countries have different needs and see a product as meeting those needs to different degrees.
  2. Customer segments: abroad, the firm may have to target a different group of buyers from the one it targets at home.
  3. Technology: the product's technology may be too advanced or too simple for the new market, depending on education levels, infrastructure, research environment, attitude to new things and local costs.
  4. Value chain: the chain of activities that turns cheap inputs into a more valuable product (Porter). If the local chain of suppliers and services is missing or inefficient, the firm cannot operate as it does at home.
  5. Product life cycle: the product may be at a different stage (new, growing, mature, declining) in each country, which changes how ready people are to buy it.

If a product offers a clear benefit, suits the local segment, technology and value chain, and arrives at the right life-cycle stage, adoption is faster.

Memory hook: "Benefits, Segments, Technology, Value chain, Life cycle" = B-S-T-V-L, "Buy Some Tea, Very Lovely".

Q6. What factors should be considered when developing a product for an international market?

Chapter 8, slide 19 (also slide 18)

Robinson listed the factors to weigh when designing a product especially for less developed countries:

  1. Level of technical skills: can local users operate and repair it?
  2. Level of labour costs: where labour is cheap, a less automated product may make sense.
  3. Level of literacy: if many cannot read, instructions and labels must rely on pictures.
  4. Level of income: the product must be affordable, maybe smaller or simpler.
  5. Level of interest rates: high borrowing costs make expensive products harder to buy on credit.
  6. Level of maintenance: products may get less care, so they must be tough and simple to service.
  7. Climate: heat, cold, dust or humidity can damage the product.
  8. Isolation: remote users far from spare parts and repair shops need a self-reliant product.
  9. Different standards: local technical and legal standards may differ from home.

Also consider the general modification factors: product standards and regulations, measurement and calibration, trademarks, climate and usage, language and symbolism, style, design and taste, technology and performance standards, and warranty and servicing.

Memory hook: "Skills, Labour, Literacy, Income, Interest, Maintenance, Climate, Isolation, Standards" = 9 "levels and conditions" of the poorer market.

Chapter 10: Promotion in International Marketing

Q8. What are the constraints in international marketing communication, and what factors affect international communication?

Chapter 10, slides 15-23

Promotion is communication: sending a message from the seller to the buyer. Abroad, some things limit what the firm can do (constraints), and some things change how the message is received (factors).

Constraints (limits):

  1. Language differences: the message may not translate well.
  2. Marketing acceptability: some promotion styles are not accepted locally.
  3. Media availability: the TV, radio or press you want may not exist there.
  4. Intermediary availability: suitable ad agencies or distributors may be missing.
  5. Activities of competitors: rivals' messages crowd and confuse yours.
  6. Government controls: laws restrict what can be advertised.

Factors (what shapes communication):

  1. Culture: in a high-context culture (meaning comes from setting and body language, not just words) the message should hint rather than state; in a low-context culture (the words carry the meaning) it should state things directly.
  2. Language: translating words does not guarantee the same meaning; meaning is often lost.
  3. Education: where literacy is low, use pictures or speech rather than writing.
  4. Economic development: decides how many can afford the product and how sophisticated it should be.
  5. Media infrastructure: media availability, reach and cost differ, so the promotion mix changes.
  6. Government regulation: rules on which product, what is said, by whom, how, when and in which medium.

Memory hook: constraints "Language, Acceptability, Media, Intermediaries, Competitors, Government"; factors "Culture, Language, Education, Economy, Media, Government".

Chapter 11: International Pricing for Profit

Q9. What is the importance of pricing in international marketing?

Chapter 11, slides 4-9

Price is the amount the buyer pays, and abroad it also carries costs the firm cannot control, such as import duties. Pricing matters because:

  1. It is the only part of the marketing mix that produces revenue: product, promotion and distribution cost money; price brings money in.
  2. It affects demand: usually inversely, meaning a higher price brings fewer buyers and a lower price more.
  3. It is linked to perceived value: buyers judge whether the product is worth the price.
  4. Reference prices: buyers compare other products against a known "reference" price (the price they expect), and price is often taken as a sign of quality.
  5. It can be changed quickly: price can be changed at short notice, depending on whether the market is free or government-regulated.
  6. It includes a bundle: the price covers not just the product but the service and other attributes around it.
  7. It is complex abroad: it must fit the firm's overall strategy, competitors, level of demand and government regulations.

So a correct international price is needed for profit, demand and image, and setting it requires research.

Memory hook: "Revenue, Demand, Value, Reference, Quick change, Bundle, Complex".

Chapter 12: Effective International Distribution

Q11. What are the alternative distribution strategies available in international markets?

Chapter 12, slides 5-13

A channel of distribution is an organised network of firms that link the producer with the final user. The alternatives are:

  1. Using an export intermediary: an export merchant in the home country buys the goods, takes ownership and ships them abroad; for the firm it feels like a home sale.
  2. Direct export: the firm exports itself, usually by appointing an agent or distributor abroad (the commonest route for small and medium firms).
  3. Exporting intellectual content: licensing (letting a foreign firm use your know-how or brand for a fee) and franchising (giving a foreign firm a full business format to copy).
  4. Manufacturing in the market: assembly (often in a foreign trade zone, a special area with low duties and government incentives), contract manufacture (a local firm makes the goods for a fee, with no rights to the output), joint ventures (shared ownership, a say in management but not control), acquisition (buying or building a plant, needing big investment), and strategic alliances.

The choice depends on six strategy factors: cost, control, commitment, nature of the market, nature of the product or service, and the firm's objective.

Memory hook: "Merchant, Myself, My know-how, Make it there"; choose by "3 Cs + market, product, objective".

Q12. What techniques are used to manage international distribution channels?

Chapter 12, slides 19-25

Once a channel is chosen, the firm must manage the agents and distributors who sell for it. Since more than half of world trade passes through agents and distributors, this matters a lot. The techniques are:

  1. Selection: carefully choosing the right agent or distributor, because the wrong one can ruin the market.
  2. Appointment: signing a clear agreement that states which products the agent represents and the exact territory it covers.
  3. Communication and control: keeping in touch and monitoring performance while creating a positive working climate for a long-term relationship.
  4. Motivation and termination: keeping the relationship "win-win" for both sides rather than each trying to take advantage, and ending it properly when needed.
  5. Wholesaling and retailing: understanding local wholesalers (who buy, sell, transport, store, finance and gather information) and local retailers, whose size and social role differ from country to country.

Memory hook: "Select, Appoint, Communicate, Motivate, then know the Wholesalers and Retailers" = the life of a partnership from hiring to ending.
