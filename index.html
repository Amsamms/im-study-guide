Mona was a mechanical engineer who had spent ten years running production lines when her father asked her to take over Wahat Foods, the family company in Alexandria that packs dates and makes date bars. Sales in Egypt had stopped growing. Then an order arrived from a shop in Germany that nobody had chased, and her father said it was time to sell abroad.

Mona first asked what selling abroad really meant. International marketing is planning and making deals across national borders. A company can do it in several ways, from simply exporting its goods to building its own operation abroad, which is called foreign direct investment. In every country it must live with four outside forces it cannot control: politics, law, culture and the economy.

She asked why firms go abroad at all. Some forces drive them out. Customers in many countries now want similar things, so one product can sell in many places. Technology and easier communication make distance matter less. And making more units makes each one cheaper, so a bigger market means lower costs. Wahat had its own reasons too: to use abroad the strengths that made it win at home, to spread its risk over more than one market, and to sell in economies growing faster than Egypt's. Two forces held her back. Markets differ, so the product usually needs some change. And governments decide who may enter their markets and how.

Her father was ethnocentric: he believed the way Wahat did things in Egypt was best and would work anywhere. A polycentric manager thinks the opposite and treats each country as different. A regiocentric manager treats a whole region as one market. A geocentric manager treats the whole world as one market. As more managers looked beyond their own country, they traded with more countries, and world trade grew. The world also made trading easier. Exchange rates were allowed to move up and down with the market, and the IMF looked after the world's money system. GATT, a world agreement, cut the taxes countries put on imports. Groups of countries such as the EU formed trade blocs and traded freely among themselves. Containers made shipping cheap, and computers let companies send orders and invoices to each other directly.

Her father asked how other companies had done it. Mona found four views. Some firms start with the step that needs the least money and commit more as they learn. Some start with countries that feel familiar, because what matters is psychic distance, how foreign a place feels, not how far away it is on a map. Some simply grab an opportunity when it appears. And some grow through relationships: first a few links abroad, then deeper ones, then a whole web of partners. The German order fitted the third view, but Mona wanted a system.

She drew her job as a wheel. In the middle was the marketing mix, the four things she controlled: the product, its price, how it is promoted, and how it reaches the buyer. Around the mix sat her home environment, which she could not control. Around that sat a ring for each foreign market, with its own economy, competitors, technology, shops, geography, culture, politics and laws. That outer ring changed from country to country.

Culture worried her most. Culture is the beliefs, customs and habits people learn from those around them. Nobody is born with it, and it changes over time. People rarely notice their own culture, while outsiders see it clearly. It tells people what behaviour is acceptable. Some things are shared everywhere, such as wanting to look good, to belong to a group, and to mourn the dead. But the details differ: technology, family, religion, taste in colour and music, and language. Culture is a collective fingerprint, a mark of identity, not of superiority. It works at three levels: the nation, the industry and the single company. The researcher Hofstede found that nations differ mainly in their values, while companies differ mainly in their practices. So Mona expected German buyers to share the same values, but each German firm to run its business in its own way.

The biggest danger, she learned, was inside her own head. The self-reference criterion is the habit of judging a foreign situation by your own culture's standards without noticing. A researcher named Lee suggested a cure in four steps. Describe the problem the way your own culture sees it. Describe it again the way the foreign culture sees it. Find where your own habits are bending the picture. Then solve the problem without them. Even what people want changes from place to place. Maslow drew human needs as a ladder. In the West it climbs from basic needs like food and safety, to belonging, to prestige, and at the top to becoming your best self. In Asia the top steps are admiration and, at the very top, status. So in the West Mona could sell a date bar as a treat for oneself, while in Asia it might sell better as a gift that earns admiration.

She also learned about context. In a high-context culture, much of the meaning comes from who says something, how and where, so people read between the lines. In a low-context culture, the words themselves carry the meaning. Hofstede's survey of IBM staff in many countries gave her a way to compare cultures on five dimensions. Power distance asks whether people accept that some have much more power than others. Uncertainty avoidance asks how threatened people feel by the unknown. Individualism against collectivism asks whether people look after themselves and their close family, or belong to tight groups. Masculinity against femininity asks how different the roles of men and women are. The fifth, added later, is long-term against short-term orientation: do people plan for the future, or look to the past and the present?

Her first real test was a negotiation with a buyer from a high-context country. She remembered the iceberg: what you see across the table is only the tip, and most of what drives the other side is hidden below. The talks went through five stages. First the two sides built trust, which took longer than she was used to. Then they opened formally and exchanged name cards. Then came the bargaining, and then the concessions and agreement, where her hurry clashed with the buyer's patience. Afterwards both sides felt the doubt that follows any big decision. She saw that cultural sensitivity means accepting the other side's ways, not giving up her own.

Then she faced two hard questions: which countries to enter, and how. Small firms often find markets by chance, through an unexpected order like Wahat's, a trip abroad or a trade fair. Experienced firms search systematically. Mona passed every country through four filters. The first looked at the country in general: its economy, its people, its politics and its geography. The second looked at the market for her kind of product: how big it was and how fast it was growing. The third looked closely at competitors, at how reliable her information was, and at the sales and profit she could expect. The last asked whether people would accept the product and how costly it would be to get in, including taxes and duties. Countries dropped out at each filter, and she was left with a priority list with Germany at the top.

To decide how to enter, she lined up the options roughly from least to most control. Every step up gives more control but costs more money and effort. Exporting through a middleman in Egypt costs least and gives least control. Exporting directly to a foreign buyer gives a little more. Licensing lets a foreign firm use her name or recipe for a fee. Franchising goes further and rents out a whole way of doing business. Contract manufacturing pays a local firm to make the goods for her. A joint venture shares ownership of a business with a local partner. Buying a foreign company, or building a new factory from nothing, gives full control at the highest cost. There was also countertrade, where goods are paid for with other goods instead of money. Mona began by exporting, one of the simplest ways in.

Then she decided what exactly to sell. A product is more than a thing with a shape and a colour. It is a bundle of physical, service and symbolic benefits. For Wahat, the physical part was the bar itself, the service part was reliable delivery, and the symbolic part was the idea of a natural Egyptian treat. Packaging had three jobs: to protect the bar, to promote it, and to make it convenient. Products also age differently in different countries. In the international product life cycle, a product is invented at home, spreads abroad, matures, gets copied worldwide, and in the end the country that invented it may start importing it. Many things push a company to change a product abroad: rules and standards, units of measurement, climate, language, taste, and warranty and service.

The big debate was whether to sell the same product everywhere or to adapt it. Standardisation saves money. It makes sense when adapting is costly and tastes are growing alike. Adaptation makes sense when competition, needs, conditions of use or laws differ. A third path is to design a global product from the start. Mona kept her bars the same and changed the pack size and the labels. She also sold dates to a few factories, and learned that industrial buyers are different. They buy only because shoppers buy their end product. There are few of them. And several people share each decision, from the user to the gatekeeper who controls what information gets through.

Then she had to name and brand it. A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol or design that identifies one seller's goods and sets them apart from competitors. It gives a lifeless product a personality and creates a bond with the buyer. A brand is legal property. It is a promise of steady quality, and a shortcut that saves the buyer a long choice every time. Strong brands bring loyalty, a higher price, market share and bargaining power. A good name is easy to say and remember and can work in other languages. Nova and Irish Mist are the classic warnings: Nova can be read in Spanish as "no va", meaning "it does not go", and "Mist" in German means dung. So before going to Germany, Mona checked how Wahat would sound there.

She weighed four brand strategies. Individual brands give each product its own name, so one failure does not hurt the rest, but each name needs its own promotion. A family brand puts the company name on everything, so new products launch cheaply, but one failure hurts them all. Several brands in one category win more shelf space. Umbrella marketing lets a new product borrow the strength of an established one, as SAAB-Scania linked its cars to its jet fighters. Mona chose a family brand and put the Wahat name on every product, accepting the risk. Over time, that name would carry brand equity, the value the name adds: whatever would vanish if the same bar were sold without it.

With the product ready, she had to tell people about it. Promotion is communication. A sender turns an idea into words and pictures and sends it through a medium. The receiver interprets it, and feedback shows whether it worked. Noise, often from competitors' messages, gets in the way. Abroad, six things make this harder: culture, language, education, economic development, the media available, and government rules. Translating the words does not carry the meaning. Mike Kelly collected hotel notices translated word for word, such as a Japanese hotel that invited guests to "take advantage of the chambermaid", meaning they could ask her for help, but in English it suggested something improper. Where few people read, the message must be pictures or speech.

She could pull or push. A pull strategy speaks to the end user, mostly through advertising, so shoppers ask the shops for the product. A push strategy speaks to the trade, the wholesalers and retailers. Abroad, sales promotion, such as trade fairs and technical seminars, matters more than at home. Mona took a stand at a food trade fair in Germany and met many buyers without appointments. She also joined a trade mission, a group of Egyptian firms visiting the market together, which opened doors to senior decision makers. Her sales staff learned to slow down to the local pace and to invest in relationships.

She learned that "made in Egypt" could help or hurt, depending on how Egypt was seen for that kind of product. When buyers do not know a product, the country's image colours their judgement like a halo. When they know the country's products well, the image sums up their past experience. For her adverts she chose the doughnut approach: a standard outer ring carrying the brand and one global message, and a centre adapted to each country.

Setting the price was harder than she expected. Price is the only part of the marketing mix that brings money in. A higher price usually means fewer buyers, but buyers also treat price as a sign of quality, so a wrong price sends the wrong signal. Her price depended on the product, the market, the channel, government rules, competition and culture. She could skim, charging a high price to the few who value the product most. She could match the market price. Or she could price low to win many buyers fast. The right price lay between a ceiling, the most the market would pay, and a floor, the lowest price that covered her costs. With no gap between them, the market was not worth entering. The Japanese approach starts from the price that wins the market and forces costs down to fit it.

She learned the terms of sale, which say where her responsibility ends. Ex-works means the buyer collects at her factory. Free on board means she pays until the goods are on the ship. Cost, insurance and freight adds the sea freight and the insurance. Delivered duty paid means she delivers to the buyer with duties paid. She also learned the payment terms, from riskiest to safest for her. With consignment she is paid only after the goods sell. With open account she ships first and invoices later. With a letter of credit a bank guarantees payment. Safest of all is cash in advance.

Then the Egyptian pound fell. In euros, her bars suddenly cost less to make. She had to decide how much of that change to pass on to her German buyer as a lower price, which is called pass-through. How much exporters pass through depends on things such as the size of the market, how many foreign firms compete there, and whether the currency rose or fell. Soon after, a German rival cut its price. She had four choices: hold her price, cut it, raise it and justify the rise, or cut it while adding value. She held her price while she watched how deep and how long the rival's cut would be.

Four things limited her pricing: competitors fixing prices together, which the law may ban; dumping, which is selling abroad below the home price; governments setting prices; and inflation, which meant reviewing prices regularly. If Wahat ever opened its own branch in Germany, she would also face transfer pricing, the price one branch charges another branch of the same company in a different country. If a branch in a high-tax country sells to a branch in a low-tax country at a low price, little profit is left to be taxed in the high-tax country.

The last piece was getting the dates to the shelves. A channel of distribution is the network of firms linking the producer with the user. Mona could sell to an export merchant in Egypt who would buy the goods and ship them. She could use an agent or distributor abroad, the most common route for small firms. Or she could manufacture abroad. A channel has a length, how many levels of middlemen it has, and a width, how many firms sit at each level. More than half of world trade passes through agents and distributors, so choosing the right one mattered most. Her agreement named the products and the territory, and she built the relationship so that both sides would win.

She soon caught a grey market: unofficial dealers buying her bars where they were cheap and reselling them where her official partners charged more. She also found that service to a customer abroad is usually worse than at home, because of distance, transport delays, customs and poor packaging. Long journeys with many stops give more chances for theft and damage, so her packaging had to protect the bars all the way. Every shipment needed four papers. The export declaration tells customs what is leaving. The bill of lading is the shipping line's receipt and proof of who owns the goods. The commercial invoice is the bill. And the certificate of origin shows where the goods were made.

On a cold morning in Hamburg, the first pallets of Wahat date bars, with German labels, reached the shelves. They were paid for by a confirmed letter of credit, which meant a second bank also guaranteed the payment, and sold by a distributor who saw the deal as good for both sides. Mona knew that without delivery, the product, the price and the adverts would have counted for nothing.

Chapter 1: Why companies go international

Why companies sell abroad, what holds them back, and how they grow there.

What is international marketing?

Making deals across national borders where each side gives something, such as goods for money. Five types:

  • Exporting: making goods at home and selling them abroad.
  • Licensing: a foreign firm pays to use your name or know-how.
  • Strategic alliance: partnering with a foreign firm.
  • Acquisition: buying a foreign company.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): putting your own money into an operation abroad.

What pushes a company abroad, and what holds it back?

Four forces push: customers in many countries need similar things, technology, economies of scale (the more you make, the cheaper each unit) and easier communication. Common reasons: a new opportunity, using the firm's strengths abroad, following customers, spreading risk, faster-growing economies, a product old at home but new elsewhere, and defence against competitors.

Two forces hold it back: markets differ, so the product usually needs changes, and governments control entry to their markets.

Trade grew because managers changed their thinking: from ethnocentric (home country first) to polycentric (each country on its own), regiocentric (by region) and geocentric (the whole world). The international money system (exchange rates, the IMF), trade agreements (GATT, EU, ASEAN, NAFTA, APEC), cheaper transport such as containers, and technology also helped.

How do companies grow abroad?

Four approaches explain it:

  • Stages: start with the entry method needing the least money and commitment, then commit more with experience.
  • Learning: a slow, step-by-step build-up, where psychic distance (how foreign a country feels) matters.
  • Contingency: take a good opportunity when it appears, even in a country that feels foreign.
  • Network: grow through relationships with other firms. Positions: international extension, penetration, integration (reaching new countries, going deeper, linking them).

What can the company control?

In the "wheel of international marketing", the firm controls only its marketing mix: product, price, promotion and channels of distribution (how the product reaches the buyer). It cannot control the environment: politics, laws, economy, competition, technology, distribution structure, geography and culture.

Chapter 3: Culture

What culture is, how it shapes buying and talking, and how to negotiate across cultures.

What is culture?

The beliefs, morals, customs and habits people learn from others. Nobody is born with it. It changes over time and is subjective (each group sees things its own way). Some things exist in every culture, such as wanting to look good, belonging to a group and mourning the dead.

A marketer studies material culture (things people make and use), social institutions (family, school, government), beliefs and religion, aesthetics (sense of beauty) and language. Culture works at three levels: nation, industry and company. Nations differ mostly in values, companies mostly in practices (ways of working).

Which ideas help a marketer read another culture?

  • Maslow's ladder of needs: everyone first needs food, then safety. The top differs: self-fulfilment in the West, status and admiration in Asia.
  • Self-reference criterion: the habit of judging a foreign situation by your own culture. Lee's four steps remove it: describe the problem in your culture's terms, then in theirs, find where your culture gets in the way, then solve it without that bias.
  • High-context vs low-context: in high-context cultures much of the meaning comes from how, where and by whom something is said, and from body language. In low-context cultures the words carry the meaning.
  • Psychic distance: how foreign a country feels (language, education, politics, development, culture). Firms prefer countries that feel close.

How do cultures differ? Hofstede's five dimensions

Hofstede's survey of IBM staff found four dimensions (ways cultures differ). A fifth came later.

  • Power distance: how far less powerful people accept unequal power.
  • Uncertainty avoidance: how threatened people feel by the unknown.
  • Individualism vs collectivism: looking after yourself and close family, or the wider group.
  • Masculine vs feminine: how different the roles of men and women are.
  • Long-term vs short-term orientation: planning for the future, or focusing on the past and present.

How does culture affect negotiation?

Negotiation has five stages. Pre-negotiation: form the team and build trust (its length depends on whether the other side is high- or low-context). Opening: get down to business. Bargaining: apply your plan. Concession and agreement: each side gives something up. Post-negotiation: doubts about whether it was the right deal (cognitive dissonance).

Take care with different ways of thinking, "face" (a person's pride and public respect), formality, harmony and emotion, and values. Most cultural differences are hidden below the surface, like an iceberg.

What does a good international marketer need?

Social sensitivity, good corporate citizenship (being a responsible member of society) and ethical behaviour. Cultural sensitivity means truly accepting other ways, not just putting up with them, while keeping your own.

Chapter 7: Choosing a country, and how to enter it

Two questions: which country, and how to enter it.

How do beginners and experienced firms choose differently? Written question

Beginners usually choose by chance ("opportunistic"): a surprise order from abroad, a chance spotted on a trip, information from others, or trade missions and trade fairs. Experienced firms choose on purpose ("systematic"), and many firms move from the first way to the second over time. For both, strategic goals now shape the choice: a strong competitive position, spreading risk, and weighing the investment against the profit. Every firm also decides: one market at a time or several, and a few markets or many.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q1.

How does a firm judge if a country is attractive? Written question

Studying each country in detail (the "grid") takes long and may be too much for a small firm. Screening instead starts with the whole world and removes unsuitable countries, like a funnel with four filters:

  1. Macro-level research (the big picture): is the country suitable in general? (economy, society, politics and law, geography)
  2. Is there a market for our kind of product? (size, growth, stage of development)
  3. Micro-level research (the detail): can we win there? (competitors, expected sales and profit)
  4. Target markets: cultural acceptance, ease and cost of entry, taxes and duties. Result: a ranked list of countries.

It also weighs membership of trade blocs (country groups with trade deals, like the EU) and competition.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q2.

Where does the information come from? Written question

Inside the firm: its own knowledge, experience and data. Outside: published references, trade journals, magazines and newspapers, government and industry advisers and support services, trade associations (groups of firms in one industry), business clubs, consultants, market research agencies and market intelligence.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q3.

How can a firm enter a country?

  • Sell from home (export-based): export through a middleman or directly, a sales office there, licensing, or franchising (a foreign firm pays to copy your brand and business model).
  • Make it there (manufacturing-based): a joint venture (a shared company with a local partner), buying a local company, or building a new factory from nothing (greenfield).
  • Partner (relationship-based): pay a local firm to make it for you (contract manufacturing), form a strategic alliance, or pay with goods instead of money (countertrade).

More control needs more money and commitment.

Chapter 8: Products and brands abroad

How products spread abroad, when to change them, and how brands and packaging work.

How do products spread, and what makes people adopt them? Written question

A product is a bundle of physical, service and symbolic features that satisfies the buyer. Diffusion is a product spreading to new countries; adoption is people there starting to buy it. There are four international product strategies: diffusion and adoption, redefining the business, leveraging capabilities (using what the firm is good at), and understanding the product market structure. For diffusion and adoption, five things matter:

  • Customer benefits: needs differ by country.
  • Customer segments (buyer groups): the target group abroad may differ from home.
  • Technology: the product may be too advanced or too simple for that market.
  • Value chain (the steps that turn materials into a product): does the country have the suppliers and services the firm needs?
  • Product life cycle (the stages of a product's life): Onkvisit and Shaw (also spelled Onkavist) give five stages: local innovation, international innovation, maturity, worldwide imitation, and reversal (the inventing country ends up importing).

Model answers: Exam tab, Q4 and Q5.

Change the product, or keep it the same?

Standardise (sell the same product everywhere) when changing is expensive, the nature of the product allows it, tastes are similar, or one version saves money in making, research, marketing and linking operations across countries. Adapt (change it for each market) because of competition, different needs and tastes, conditions of use, government pressure and laws. Often changed: standards, measurement units, trademarks, climate fit, language and symbols, style and taste, technology, warranty and service. A third way: a global product designed for all markets from the start.

What matters when designing for a less developed country? Written question

Robinson's nine factors: technical skills, labour costs, literacy (how many people can read), income, interest rates, maintenance, climate, isolation and different standards.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q6.

How are industrial products different?

Businesses buy them to make other products. Demand is derived (it comes from consumer demand), is often reciprocal (buyers and sellers buy from each other), and buyers are few. Several people share the decision: initiator (starts it), decider, influencer, gatekeeper (controls the information), user and purchaser.

Which brand strategy? Written question

A brand is a name, sign or design that identifies a seller's product and sets it apart from competitors.

  • Individual brands: a separate name per product, so each gets the best-fitting name; a failure does not hurt the company name, but each needs heavy promotion.
  • Family brand: the company name combined with each product name; new products launch cheaply and sell at once, but one failure hurts them all.
  • Multibrand: several brands in the same category; more shelf space, catches customers who switch, creates excitement and competition inside the company, serves different buyer groups.
  • Umbrella: a new product borrows the strength of a known brand (SAAB-Scania linked its cars to its jet fighters in advertising).

A good brand name suggests the benefit, is easy to say and remember, stands out and works in other languages. Brand equity is the extra value the name adds. Packaging protects, promotes and makes the product convenient.

Chapter 10: Promotion abroad

How to get a message across abroad, and which tools to use.

How does communication work?

A sender encodes a message (puts it into words and images) and sends it through a medium, such as TV, a letter or a billboard. The receiver decodes it (works out what it means). Feedback shows whether it worked. Noise, mainly competitors' messages at the same time, gets in the way.

Why is communication harder abroad? Written question

Constraints (limits): language, what marketing is acceptable, which media and middlemen exist, competitors and government controls.

Factors:

  • Culture: in high-context cultures hint; in low-context cultures say it directly.
  • Language: a word-for-word translation loses the meaning and causes famous mistakes.
  • Education: where few people read, use pictures and sound.
  • Economic development: who can afford the product.
  • Media infrastructure: which media exist and what they cost.
  • Government rules: what may be advertised, what is said, by whom, how, when and in which medium.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q8.

Pull or push?

  • Pull (aimed directly at the final buyer): advertising (print, TV, cinema, radio, global media, the internet) and public relations (news releases, sponsorships, loyalty programmes).
  • Push: sales promotion, which matters more abroad than at home (trade shows, technical seminars), and personal selling, face to face (trade missions are the most common abroad, plus a salesforce, brochures and direct mail).

Trade missions (group business visits abroad) open doors to senior decision makers and regulators, bring information and publicity, and build relationships. But competitors may join, planning takes long, it is often by invitation only, and you meet fewer contacts than at a trade show.

Does "made in" matter?

Yes. A country's image can help or hurt a product. Halo: buyers judge an unknown product by the country's image. Summary: buyers build the country's image from its products they already know.

Same ads everywhere?

Globalisation pushes for the same ads everywhere, to save money. The doughnut approach mixes both: a fixed outer ring (brand name and global message) with a local centre for each country.

Chapter 11: Pricing abroad

Why price matters, how to set it in another country, and what limits it.

Why does price matter? Written question

Price is the only part of the marketing mix that brings in money. Usually a higher price means fewer buyers. It signals quality and can be changed quickly. Abroad, competitors, government rules, culture, channel, location and product type make it harder.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q9.

How is the price set?

  • Strategies: skimming (start high), market pricing (match the usual price), penetration (start low).
  • Cost-based or market-based: add a profit to the full cost, or price from what the market pays. Some count only marginal cost (the extra cost of making more).
  • Ceiling and floor: the highest price buyers accept and the lowest price that covers cost. No gap between them means leave the market, unless things should improve.
  • Japanese approach: decide the winning price first, then cut cost to fit it.

Terms of sale say where the seller's cost and risk end: from ex-works (the buyer collects at the factory) to delivered duty paid (the seller delivers and pays the import tax).

Common terms of payment: consignment (paid after the goods are sold), open account (goods first, bill later), documents against acceptance, documents against payment, letter of credit (a bank promises to pay), confirmed letter of credit, and cash in advance.

What limits the price?

  • Laws against restrictive practices, such as price fixing and cartels (firms agreeing prices together).
  • Dumping (selling abroad below the normal price): predatory (to push out rivals), sporadic (now and then) or unintentional.
  • Devaluation and revaluation (a country lowers or raises its currency's value).
  • Administration pricing: the government sets the price.
  • Inflation (rising prices).

Global or local prices? Written question

  • Standard world price: the same price everywhere.
  • Market-differentiated price: each country gets its own price.
  • Modified pricing policy: a middle way between the two.

Transfer pricing is the price one branch of a multinational (a firm with operations in many countries) charges another. Selling cheaply from a high-tax branch to a low-tax branch leaves little profit (and tax) in the high-tax country. It happens only with branches abroad, not simple exporting.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q10.

Chapter 12: Distribution abroad

How products reach foreign buyers, and how to manage the partners who sell them.

What is a channel?

The network of firms linking the producer to the final user. Distribution is the physical flow of goods through it. A good channel is an advantage; a bad one causes problems.

What are the options? Written question

  • Export merchant: a home-country trader buys the goods and ships them abroad. The merchant owns them, so for the firm it is like a sale at home.
  • Direct export: the firm exports itself through a foreign agent or distributor; the commonest way for small and medium firms.
  • Licensing and franchising: exporting know-how instead of goods.
  • Making it abroad: assembly (often in a foreign trade zone with government incentives), contract manufacture (a firm there is paid to make it but does not own it), joint venture (a say in management, not full control), acquisition (buying or building a plant), strategic alliance.

The choice depends on cost, control, commitment, the market, the product and the firm's goals.

Model answers: Exam tab, Q11 and Q13.

How do you manage channel partners? Written question

  1. Selection: choose carefully; agents and distributors handle over half of world trade.
  2. Appointment: a written agreement stating which products and which area.
  3. Communication and control: keep a positive climate for a long relationship.
  4. Motivation and termination: keep it win-win for both sides.
  5. Wholesaling and retailing: wholesalers (firms between the maker and the shops) buy, sell, transport, store, finance and gather information. Retailing (selling to the final buyer) differs a lot between countries.

Model answer: Exam tab, Q12.

What global problems affect distribution?

Standardisation (doing things the same way everywhere), either of how the firm is managed (process) or of the marketing mix (programme). Grey markets: unofficial sellers buy the product cheaply in one country and resell it in a dearer one below the official price.

What about the physical side?

Customer service abroad is usually lower than at home (distance, mistakes, poor packaging, delays, customs). The firm must also plan packaging, transport, warehousing (storage) and four key documents: export declaration, bill of lading (the shipping receipt), commercial invoice (the bill) and certificate of origin (proof of where it was made).

Chapter 7: International Market Selection and Entry

Q1. What are the different problems faced in selecting international markets by firms inexperienced and experienced in international business?

Chapter 7, slides 2, 4-8, 10-12, 21-23

Choosing which foreign country to sell in is one of the two most critical questions a firm faces when going abroad (the other is how to enter). A wrong choice can hurt profits not only abroad but also at home, and the problems differ between a beginner firm and an experienced one.

PointInexperienced firm (beginner, often small or medium)Experienced firm (often large)
How the market is foundOpportunistic: it reacts to chance, such as an unexpected order, a market noticed on a trip, tips from third parties, or a trade fairSystematic: it searches and compares markets on purpose, step by step
Pace of entryUsually incremental: one market at a timeCan enter several markets at the same time
SpreadConcentrated: focuses on a few marketsCan be diversified: spreads over many markets
Analysis toolsThe country-by-country grid is too slow and costly for its small management team, so it needs a simple screening method that eliminates unsuitable markets step by stepCan afford the grid that compares each market's attractiveness with its own competitive strength
InformationLittle market information and little experience inside the firmMore internal knowledge, company data and paid research

For both types of firm, the choice is shaped by:

  1. Strategic objectives: building a competitive position, spreading (transferring) risk, and weighing the investment in a market against the profit it brings.
  2. Company size: bigger firms have more people and money to study and enter markets.
  3. Company strategy: for example selling off surplus production, wanting steady sales with growth, a target profit percentage abroad, or growing market share.
  4. Availability of market information: where data is poor, selection is harder for everyone.

In short, beginners select by chance and with little data, while experienced firms select by system, but both must link the choice to their goals and resources.

Memory hook: beginner = "chance, one by one, few, screen"; expert = "system, many at once, spread, grid".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: how does a company decide which foreign country to sell in, and why is this harder for a newcomer than for a veteran?

The beginner. Example: a small Egyptian juice maker has never exported. One day a buyer in Kenya emails asking for a shipment, so Kenya becomes its first foreign market. Nobody studied Kenya; the chance came to the firm. That is opportunistic selection (reacting to luck). Because the firm is small, with few managers, little money and little data, it enters one country at a time and stays in a few countries. It cannot afford a big study of every country, so it uses screening: start with all countries and cross out the unsuitable ones step by step (too poor, too far, too risky) until a few are left.

The experienced firm. A big company that already sells abroad searches on purpose (systematic selection). It has the people and the money to enter several countries at once and to spread over many. It can afford the grid: a table that scores every country on two things, "how attractive is this market?" and "how strong are we there?", then picks the best mix.

What steers both of them: their goals (win a strong position, spread risk so one bad country does not sink the firm, and weigh money invested against profit), their size, their strategy (for example getting rid of extra production) and whether information about the market even exists.

To remember: beginners pick by chance, slowly and with little data; experienced firms pick by system, widely and with more data.

Q2. How can a country's attractiveness as an international market be assessed in terms of market potential, membership of trading blocs, competitive intensity, and entry barriers?

Chapter 7, slides 2, 9-20 (trading blocs: Chapter 1, slide 10)

Country attractiveness means how worthwhile a foreign market is for the firm. It is judged by passing all countries through a series of filters, moving from general facts to facts about the specific product, until a short priority list of target markets remains.

  1. Market potential (macro level, filter 1): general facts about the country: economic statistics, social structure, political and legal environment, and geography. Weak countries are rejected early.
  2. Market size indicators: geography (area, climate, terrain), people (total population, growth rate, age mix, density) and money (total and per-person national product (GNP), income and its growth, disposable income (money left after tax), how evenly income is spread).
  3. Market for this product (filter 2): growth trends of similar products, market size, the product's life-cycle stage there (introduction, growth, maturity, decline), and whether market data exists.
  4. Trading bloc membership: whether the country belongs to a trade group such as GATT (the world agreement to cut import taxes), the EU, ASEAN, NAFTA or APEC, which can open (or close) access to a bigger group of countries.
  5. Competitive intensity (micro level, filter 3): existing and potential competitors, how reliable the information is, expected sales and profit potential.
  6. Company sales potential: local import laws, competition, the local distribution set-up, physical distance from home, and language and cultural differences.
  7. Entry barriers (filter 4, implementation): whether the culture accepts the product, how easy and costly entry is, and taxes and duties.

The countries that survive all filters form a portfolio (a chosen set) of the most attractive markets, ranked in a country priority list.

Memory hook: "General country, then product market, then competition, then barriers" = 4 filters from wide to narrow.

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: how does a firm decide whether a country is worth entering?

The idea is a funnel (a series of sieves). Think of hiring: you get 200 CVs, first you drop everyone without the right degree, then you look at experience, then you interview, and finally one or two people are left. Choosing a country works the same way: all countries go in at the top, and four "filters" remove the weak ones until a short ranked list remains.

  1. Filter 1, the country in general (macro level = the big picture): is the economy healthy, how many people live there, how rich are they, is the government stable, are the laws friendly, what is the geography and climate? This is market potential: how much could be sold there at all.
  2. Filter 2, the market for MY product: how big is the market for this kind of product, is it growing, and is the product new there or already old?
  3. Filter 3, competition (micro level = the close-up): who already sells there, how strong are they, and how much could we realistically sell and earn? This is competitive intensity: how crowded the market is.
  4. Filter 4, entry barriers: the obstacles to getting in. Will the culture accept the product, how hard and costly is entry, and how high are the taxes and duties (a duty is a tax charged on imported goods)?

Trading blocs: a trading bloc is a club of countries that trade with each other at low or zero import tax, such as the EU. If a country is in a bloc, entering it can open the door to the whole club, which makes it more attractive.

Example: an Egyptian paint company looking at Africa might drop very small or unstable countries first, then countries where little paint is sold, then countries full of strong rivals, and finally countries with very high import duties. What is left is its priority list.

To remember: four sieves from wide to narrow: the country, the product's market, the competition, the barriers.

Q3. What are the information sources used in international market selection?

Chapter 7, slide 23

To choose a foreign market a firm needs facts, which come from two places: inside the firm and outside it.

Internal sources (inside the firm):

  1. Knowledge and experience within the firm: what managers and staff already know from past dealings, travel or previous exports.
  2. Company data: the firm's own records, such as sales figures, customer enquiries and costs.

External sources (outside the firm):

  1. Published references: reference books and directories about countries and markets.
  2. Trade journals, magazines and newspapers: news and reports about industries and countries.
  3. Government or industry advisers and support services: export promotion bodies and official advisers who help firms going abroad.
  4. Trade associations, business clubs, consultants, market research agencies and market intelligence: organisations that collect or sell information and contacts.

Beginner firms lean more on external, often free, sources; experienced firms also have rich internal knowledge.

Memory hook: "Inside = what we know + our data; Outside = read it, ask government, ask associations and agencies".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: where does a company get the facts it needs to pick a foreign market?

There are only two places: inside the company and outside it.

Inside (internal sources): what the staff already know (a manager who worked in the Gulf for years, a salesperson who visited a trade fair) and the company's own records (past sales, enquiries from foreign buyers, costs).

Outside (external sources):

  • Published references: reference books and directories that list countries, companies and market facts.
  • Trade journals, magazines and newspapers: a trade journal is a specialist magazine for one industry (for example Oil & Gas Journal for petroleum), full of market news, prices and who is expanding where.
  • Government or industry advisers: official bodies whose job is to help firms export, such as an export promotion council.
  • Trade associations, business clubs, consultants and market research agencies: groups that collect information, or companies you pay to research a market for you.

A beginner firm knows little itself, so it relies mostly on outside sources, many of them free. An experienced firm also has a lot of knowledge inside.

To remember: inside = what we know and what our records show; outside = what we read, who advises us, and who we pay.

Chapter 8: Modifying Products for International Markets

Q4. How do products spread to international markets?

Chapter 8, slides 4-5, 11-16

A product spreads abroad through diffusion (moving from country to country) and adoption (people in each new country starting to buy it). The international product strategies behind this are:

  1. Product diffusion and adoption: the product moves into new countries as customers there see a benefit in it (explained further in Q5).
  2. Redefining the business: the firm rethinks what business it is in, so the product can fit the needs of new markets.
  3. Leveraging capabilities: using what the firm already has to enter new markets more cheaply: shared physical assets, contract manufacturing (paying another firm to make the goods), shared outside relationships, established networks, shared information and expertise, and spreading development costs.
  4. Understanding product market structure: knowing where the market's boundaries lie in each country (the product's use, what the category includes, what competitors do), how to differentiate and brand the product, and the market's life-cycle stage.

The spread usually follows the international product life cycle (Onkvisit and Shaw, spelled "Onkavist" on the slide), five stages:

  1. Local innovation: the product is invented and sold at home.
  2. International innovation: it starts being exported.
  3. Maturity: demand abroad grows and steadies.
  4. Worldwide imitation: producers in other countries copy it.
  5. Reversal: the original home country ends up importing the product from the copiers.

Memory hook: strategies "Diffuse, Redefine, Leverage, Understand"; life cycle "Local, International, Mature, Imitate, Reverse".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: how does a product travel from its home country into other countries?

Two words do the work. Diffusion is the spreading of a product from country to country, like news spreading from person to person. Adoption is the moment people in a new country actually start buying it.

What the firm does to help the spread:

  • Redefine the business: rethink what the firm really sells so the product fits new markets.
  • Leverage capabilities: "leverage" means using what you already have to get more. The firm uses its existing factories, partners, networks, knowledge and past development spending, so entering a new country costs less.
  • Understand the product market structure: in each country, learn what people use the product for, what they see as its competitors, and how to brand it there.

The international product life cycle (five stages). A classic illustration is the television set: (1) local innovation, it is invented and sold at home; (2) international innovation, it starts being exported; (3) maturity, demand abroad grows and settles; (4) worldwide imitation, factories in other countries learn to make it, often cheaper; (5) reversal, the inventing country ends up importing it from the copiers. That last twist is the one examiners love.

To remember: the product is born at home, travels abroad, gets copied, and finally comes back home as an import.

Q5. What factors influence the adoption of products by consumers in international markets?

Chapter 8, slides 5-11

Adoption means customers in a foreign country accepting and starting to buy a product. Five factors decide how fast and how far this happens:

  1. Customer benefits: people in different countries have different needs and see a product as meeting those needs to different degrees.
  2. Customer segments: abroad, the firm may have to target a different group of buyers from the one it targets at home.
  3. Technology: the product's technology may be too advanced or too simple for the new market, depending on education levels, infrastructure, research environment, attitude to new things and local costs.
  4. Value chain: the chain of activities that turns cheap inputs into a more valuable product (Porter). If the local chain of suppliers and services is missing or inefficient, the firm cannot operate as it does at home.
  5. Product life cycle: the product may be at a different stage (new, growing, mature, declining) in each country, which changes how ready people are to buy it.

If a product offers a clear benefit, suits the local segment, technology and value chain, and arrives at the right life-cycle stage, adoption is faster.

Memory hook: "Benefits, Segments, Technology, Value chain, Life cycle" = B-S-T-V-L, "Buy Some Tea, Very Lovely".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: why do people in one country rush to buy a product while people in another country ignore it?

  1. Customer benefits: does the product solve a real problem for people THERE? An air conditioner is a must in Cairo and a waste of money in a cold country.
  2. Customer segments: a segment is one group of similar buyers. The group that buys your product at home may not exist abroad, so you may need to sell to a different group.
  3. Technology: the product may be too advanced or too simple for the country. Example: a machine that needs highly trained engineers and a stable power grid will not catch on where both are scarce.
  4. Value chain: all the steps and partners needed to make the product and get it to the customer: suppliers, transport, shops, repair services. If these are missing abroad, the product struggles. Example: an electric car will not sell where there are no charging stations.
  5. Product life cycle: the product may be brand new in one country and old news in another, so people are at different levels of readiness to buy it.

To remember: Benefit, Segment, Technology, Value chain, Life cycle ("Buy Some Tea, Very Lovely"). The better the fit on all five, the faster people adopt it.

Q6. What factors should be considered when developing a product for an international market?

Chapter 8, slide 19 (also slide 18)

Robinson listed the factors to weigh when designing a product especially for less developed countries:

  1. Level of technical skills: can local users operate and repair it?
  2. Level of labour costs: where labour is cheap, a less automated product may make sense.
  3. Level of literacy: if many cannot read, instructions and labels must rely on pictures.
  4. Level of income: the product must be affordable, maybe smaller or simpler.
  5. Level of interest rates: high borrowing costs make expensive products harder to buy on credit.
  6. Level of maintenance: products may get less care, so they must be tough and simple to service.
  7. Climate: heat, cold, dust or humidity can damage the product.
  8. Isolation: remote users far from spare parts and repair shops need a self-reliant product.
  9. Different standards: local technical and legal standards may differ from home.

Also consider the general modification factors: product standards and regulations, measurement and calibration, trademarks, climate and usage, language and symbolism, style, design and taste, technology and performance standards, and warranty and servicing.

Memory hook: "Skills, Labour, Literacy, Income, Interest, Maintenance, Climate, Isolation, Standards" = 9 "levels and conditions" of the poorer market.

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: if you design a product for a poorer country, what must you think about?

Imagine you are designing a water pump for villages. Robinson's nine factors become simple questions:

  1. Technical skills: can local people operate and repair it? Then keep it simple.
  2. Labour costs: if workers are cheap, a hand-operated pump may make more sense than an automatic one.
  3. Literacy: if many people cannot read, use pictures for the instructions.
  4. Income: people have little money, so make it cheaper, smaller or simpler.
  5. Interest rates: if loans are expensive, people cannot buy in instalments, so the price must be low enough to pay at once.
  6. Maintenance: it may get little care, so make it tough and easy to service.
  7. Climate: heat, dust and humidity must not damage it.
  8. Isolation: villages far from spare parts and repair shops need a product that keeps working on its own.
  9. Different standards: the country's technical and legal rules may differ from home (voltage, plugs, safety rules).

Beyond poorer countries, any product may need changes for regulations, units of measurement, trademarks, language and symbols on the pack, local taste and style, and warranty and servicing.

To remember: design for the users' skills, pockets, reading level, weather and distance from help.

Q7. What are the different brand strategies used in international markets?

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: how should a company name its products when it sells in many countries?

A brand is the name, logo or design that shows who made a product and sets it apart from rivals. There are four ways to organise brands:

  • Individual brands, each product its own name. Example: Procter & Gamble sells Pampers, Gillette and Ariel; most shoppers do not know they come from one company. Plus: each product gets the name that suits it, and if one fails or has a scandal the others are untouched. Minus: every name starts from zero, so each needs expensive advertising.

  • Umbrella, a new product shelters under a famous name. The slide example: SAAB reminded car buyers that it also builds jet fighters, so its cars borrowed that image of top engineering.

To remember: Individual = separate names; Family = company name on all; Multi = rival brands, same owner; Umbrella = borrow a star's reputation.

Chapter 10: Promotion in International Marketing

Q8. What are the constraints in international marketing communication, and what factors affect international communication?

Chapter 10, slides 15-23

Promotion is communication: sending a message from the seller to the buyer. Abroad, some things limit what the firm can do (constraints), and some things change how the message is received (factors).

Constraints (limits):

  1. Language differences: the message may not translate well.
  2. Marketing acceptability: some promotion styles are not accepted locally.
  3. Media availability: the TV, radio or press you want may not exist there.
  4. Intermediary availability: suitable ad agencies or distributors may be missing.
  5. Activities of competitors: rivals' messages crowd and confuse yours.
  6. Government controls: laws restrict what can be advertised.

Factors (what shapes communication):

  1. Culture: in a high-context culture (meaning comes from setting and body language, not just words) the message should hint rather than state; in a low-context culture (the words carry the meaning) it should state things directly.
  2. Language: translating words does not guarantee the same meaning; meaning is often lost.
  3. Education: where literacy is low, use pictures or speech rather than writing.
  4. Economic development: decides how many can afford the product and how sophisticated it should be.
  5. Media infrastructure: media availability, reach and cost differ, so the promotion mix changes.
  6. Government regulation: rules on which product, what is said, by whom, how, when and in which medium.

Memory hook: constraints "Language, Acceptability, Media, Intermediaries, Competitors, Government"; factors "Culture, Language, Education, Economy, Media, Government".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: what makes advertising and promotion harder abroad?

Two lists. Constraints are walls that limit what you can do. Factors are things that change how your message is understood.

Constraints (the walls): the language does not translate well; the style of promotion is not acceptable locally; the TV channel, newspaper or website you want does not exist there; there is no good ad agency or distributor to help; competitors' messages drown yours out; and the government forbids certain ads (many countries, for example, restrict tobacco advertising).

Factors (what shapes the message):

  • Culture: in a high-context culture, much of the meaning comes from the setting, tone and relationship rather than the words, so an ad should hint and suggest. In a low-context culture, the words carry the meaning, so the ad should say things plainly.
  • Language: translating the words is not enough; the meaning can change or get lost. A clever slogan in English can sound odd or rude in Arabic.
  • Education: where many people cannot read, use pictures, sound and video instead of text.
  • Economic development: decides how many people can afford the product and how fancy the message should be.
  • Media infrastructure: which media exist, how many people they reach, and what they cost.
  • Government regulation: rules on what product can be advertised, what may be said, by whom, how, when and where.

To remember: constraints are the walls around you; factors are the lenses your message passes through. Language and government appear in both lists.

Chapter 11: International Pricing for Profit

Q9. What is the importance of pricing in international marketing?

Chapter 11, slides 4-9

Price is the amount the buyer pays, and abroad it also carries costs the firm cannot control, such as import duties. Pricing matters because:

  1. It is the only part of the marketing mix that produces revenue: product, promotion and distribution cost money; price brings money in.
  2. It affects demand: usually inversely, meaning a higher price brings fewer buyers and a lower price more.
  3. It is linked to perceived value: buyers judge whether the product is worth the price.
  4. Reference prices: buyers compare other products against a known "reference" price (the price they expect), and price is often taken as a sign of quality.
  5. It can be changed quickly: price can be changed at short notice, depending on whether the market is free or government-regulated.
  6. It includes a bundle: the price covers not just the product but the service and other attributes around it.
  7. It is complex abroad: it must fit the firm's overall strategy, competitors, level of demand and government regulations.

So a correct international price is needed for profit, demand and image, and setting it requires research.

Memory hook: "Revenue, Demand, Value, Reference, Quick change, Bundle, Complex".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: why does getting the price right matter so much abroad?

  • It is the only part of marketing that brings money in. Making the product, advertising it and shipping it all cost money; the price is where money comes back.
  • It moves demand. Usually higher price means fewer buyers, lower price means more.
  • It must match perceived value: what the buyer feels the product is worth.
  • Reference prices: buyers carry a "normal price" in their heads. If you expect a coffee to cost about 50 pounds, 200 feels expensive, and 5 makes you wonder what is wrong with it. That is why price is often read as a sign of quality.
  • It can change fast: unlike the product or the distribution network, a price can be changed at short notice, unless the government regulates it.
  • It is a bundle: the price pays for more than the item: delivery, warranty, service and other extras.
  • It is more complicated abroad: the firm must fit its overall strategy, local competitors, local demand and local government rules, plus costs it cannot control such as import duties.

To remember: price is the money maker, the demand dial and the quality signal, and abroad it is harder to set.

Q10. What is the difference between global and local pricing strategies?

Chapter 11, slides 38-40

A global pricing strategy uses one approach to price across all countries; a local strategy sets price country by country. There are three options:

OptionWhat it meansGlobal or local?
Standard world pricesThe same price everywhere, adjusted only for exchange rates and transportGlobal
Market differentiated pricesEach country gets its own price based on local conditions such as demand, competition and incomeLocal
Modified pricing policyA middle path: a common base or guideline set centrally, with some local adjustment allowedMix of both

Related to global pricing is transfer pricing: the price one part of a multinational charges another part in a different country. For example, goods are sent at a low price from a subsidiary in a high-tax country to one in a low-tax country, so little profit (and tax) appears in the high-tax country. It happens with international investment, not with simple exporting.

Memory hook: "Same, Different, Mixed" + "transfer = moving profit to the low-tax side".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: should a company charge the same price everywhere, or a different price in each country?

Three options, from "one price" to "many prices":

  • Standard world price (global): the same price everywhere, changed only for exchange rates and shipping. Simple, and customers cannot buy cheap in one country and resell in another, but it ignores that people in poorer countries can pay less.
  • Market differentiated price (local): each country gets its own price based on local demand, competition and income. Fits each market, but harder to manage.
  • Modified pricing policy (a mix): head office sets a base price or a range, and each country may adjust within it.

Transfer pricing is a different thing: the price one branch of a multinational charges ANOTHER branch of the same company in another country. A simple illustration: a group has a factory in a country with high company tax and a sales branch in a country with low tax. If the factory "sells" its goods to the sister branch very cheaply, little profit appears in the high-tax country and most appears in the low-tax one, so the group pays less tax overall. This needs branches abroad, so it does not happen with simple exporting.

To remember: global = same price, local = different prices, modified = a mix; transfer pricing = moving profit between your own branches.

Chapter 12: Effective International Distribution

Q11. What are the alternative distribution strategies available in international markets?

Chapter 12, slides 5-13

A channel of distribution is an organised network of firms that link the producer with the final user. The alternatives are:

  1. Using an export intermediary: an export merchant in the home country buys the goods, takes ownership and ships them abroad; for the firm it feels like a home sale.
  2. Direct export: the firm exports itself, usually by appointing an agent or distributor abroad (the commonest route for small and medium firms).
  3. Exporting intellectual content: licensing (letting a foreign firm use your know-how or brand for a fee) and franchising (giving a foreign firm a full business format to copy).
  4. Manufacturing in the market: assembly (often in a foreign trade zone, a special area with low duties and government incentives), contract manufacture (a local firm makes the goods for a fee, with no rights to the output), joint ventures (shared ownership, a say in management but not control), acquisition (buying or building a plant, needing big investment), and strategic alliances.

The choice depends on six strategy factors: cost, control, commitment, nature of the market, nature of the product or service, and the firm's objective.

Memory hook: "Merchant, Myself, My know-how, Make it there"; choose by "3 Cs + market, product, objective".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: what routes can a company use to get its product to buyers in another country?

A distribution channel is the route a product travels from the factory to the final user, like a pipeline with stations along the way. The routes, from least effort to most:

  1. Use an export merchant at home: a trader in your own country buys your goods, becomes their owner, and ships them abroad himself. For you it feels like a normal home sale.
  2. Direct export: you export yourself and appoint an agent or distributor in the foreign country to sell for you. This is the most common route for small and medium firms.
  3. Export your know-how instead of goods: licensing lets a foreign firm use your technology or brand for a fee; franchising gives it your whole way of doing business to copy (think of a restaurant chain).
  4. Make it in the foreign country: assembly (put parts together there, often in a special low-tax zone the government offers to attract investors); contract manufacture (pay a local factory to make it; the factory has no rights to the product); joint venture (share ownership with a local partner, so you have a say but not full control); acquisition (buy a local company or build your own plant, the most expensive); and strategic alliances (partnerships).

How to choose: cost, control, commitment (how much money and effort you tie up), the nature of the market, the nature of the product, and what the firm wants to achieve.

To remember: sell to a merchant, sell it yourself, sell your know-how, or make it there.

Q12. What techniques are used to manage international distribution channels?

Chapter 12, slides 19-25

Once a channel is chosen, the firm must manage the agents and distributors who sell for it. Since more than half of world trade passes through agents and distributors, this matters a lot. The techniques are:

  1. Selection: carefully choosing the right agent or distributor, because the wrong one can ruin the market.
  2. Appointment: signing a clear agreement that states which products the agent represents and the exact territory it covers.
  3. Communication and control: keeping in touch and monitoring performance while creating a positive working climate for a long-term relationship.
  4. Motivation and termination: keeping the relationship "win-win" for both sides rather than each trying to take advantage, and ending it properly when needed.
  5. Wholesaling and retailing: understanding local wholesalers (who buy, sell, transport, store, finance and gather information) and local retailers, whose size and social role differ from country to country.

Memory hook: "Select, Appoint, Communicate, Motivate, then know the Wholesalers and Retailers" = the life of a partnership from hiring to ending.

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: once a company has agents and distributors abroad, how does it manage them well?

Think of it like hiring and managing an employee who works in another country. More than half of world trade goes through agents and distributors, so this matters.

  1. Selection (hiring): choose the right partner carefully; a bad one can ruin your name in that market.
  2. Appointment (the contract): sign a clear agreement saying exactly which products they sell and which area (territory) they cover.
  3. Communication and control (supervision): keep in regular contact, check their results, and build a friendly working climate for the long term.
  4. Motivation and termination (keeping them keen, and ending properly): make the deal "win-win", so both sides gain and neither tries to cheat the other, and if it must end, end it properly.
  5. Wholesaling and retailing (knowing the local shops): understand the local middlemen. A wholesaler buys in bulk and sells to shops, and also transports, stores, finances and gathers information. A retailer is the shop that sells to the public; how big shops are and the role they play differ by country.

Example: an Egyptian food company appoints a distributor in Saudi Arabia for its biscuits in the Riyadh region only, calls every month to review sales, pays a good margin so the distributor pushes the product, and studies which supermarkets and small shops dominate there.

To remember: hire, sign, supervise, motivate (or part ways), and know the local wholesalers and shops.

Q13. What are the different international distribution channels? Compare the available alternatives.

Memory hook: "the deeper you go (merchant to acquisition), the more control and the more money".

Plain English (for a cold reader)

What the question really asks: list the ways of getting a product to foreign buyers and compare them.

The key idea is one trade-off: control against commitment. Control is how much say you have over how your product is sold. Commitment is how much money and effort you tie up. You cannot get high control cheaply.

A simple picture is getting to work: a taxi is cheap to start with and needs no investment, but you do not control the route (that is the export merchant). Hiring a driver gives more say for more cost (agent or distributor). Buying your own car gives full control but a big upfront cost (acquisition or your own plant). The table above runs the same way, from the merchant (least control, least money) down to acquisition (most control, most money), with licensing, assembly and joint ventures in between.

Channels are also described by five measures:

  • Length: how many steps between factory and buyer (factory to wholesaler to shop to customer is long).
  • Width: how many middlemen at each step (one distributor for the whole country is narrow).
  • Density: how much of the market you cover (in every corner shop, or only in big cities).
  • Alignment: who leads the channel and makes the decisions.
  • Logistics: how the goods physically move: shipping, storage, delivery.

To remember: the deeper you go into the country, the more control you get and the more money you put in.
